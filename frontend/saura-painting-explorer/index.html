Historical Overview

Saura painting is one of India's oldest surviving tribal art traditions, practiced by the Saura (also known as Lanjia Saura) tribe of Odisha's Eastern Ghats. The art form dates back thousands of years, with roots in prehistoric rock shelter paintings found in the region. The Saura people are one of Odisha's Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs), and their wall paintings are a living continuation of some of humanity's earliest artistic expressions.

Traditionally painted on the mud-and-wattle walls of Saura homes during rituals, festivals, and life events, these paintings serve as both decorative art and sacred documentation. Each painting tells a story — a harvest celebration, a wedding, a hunting expedition, or a propitiation of the tribe's pantheon of deities. The signature style features rows of small, simplified human figures (often depicted as stick-like forms with round heads) arranged in narrative sequences.

In recent decades, Saura art has been transferred from walls to canvas and paper, bringing it to galleries and collectors worldwide. Despite this transition, the core visual vocabulary — the iconic human figures, the fish-scale borders, and the earthy palette — remains unchanged, preserving a continuous link from prehistory to the present day.

Region of Origin

📍 Core Region

The Eastern Ghats of Odisha, primarily the Rayagada and Gajapati districts. The Saura villages are nestled in the forested hill tracts of these districts.

🌳 Landscape

Dense tropical deciduous forests, rugged hills, and streams characterize the Saura homeland. The natural pigments used in the paintings — red laterite soil, white river clay, and black charcoal — are all sourced from this landscape.

🏛️ Cultural Context

Saura paintings are created on the inner and outer walls of homes (known as italu). The Lanjia Saura are named for their distinctive lanjia (loincloth), and their wall art is an integral part of their animistic religious practice.

🔖 GI Tag

Saura painting received a Geographical Indication (GI) tag, recognizing its unique origin and protecting it from imitation. This has helped elevate the art form's economic value for the tribe.
