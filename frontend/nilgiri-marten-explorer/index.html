Introduction

The Nilgiri Marten (Martes gwatkinsii) is India's only endemic marten species, found exclusively in the hill forests of the Western Ghats in southern India. Unusual among martens worldwide for being active by day rather than night, this agile, tree-climbing hunter is a rarely seen but distinctive resident of the region's cool, misty shola-grassland landscapes.

Scientific Name

Martes gwatkinsii (Horsfield, 1851). Named after Gwatkin, the species was first described in the mid-19th century. It is sometimes listed under the synonym Charronia gwatkinsii, though Martes gwatkinsii remains the accepted scientific name.

Physical Characteristics

  • Brown coat with a distinctive yellow or orange throat patch.
  • Similar in size and appearance to the Yellow-throated Marten, but slightly larger, with a differently structured skull.
  • Body length: roughly 55–65 cm; tail length: roughly 40–45 cm.
  • Average body weight: around 2.1 kg.
  • A high basal metabolic rate typical of martens, supporting an active, energetic lifestyle.

Distribution

The Nilgiri Marten is endemic to the Western Ghats mountain range of southern India, with its known range severely fragmented across the Nilgiris and adjoining hill ranges. Its total extent of occurrence is estimated at under 20,000 km², and a 2022 study recorded the species' presence in Wayanad for the first time, expanding its documented range.

States: Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu.

Habitat

Nilgiri Martens are found mainly in the montane shola forests and adjoining grasslands of the Western Ghats, at elevations between roughly 300 and 1,200 metres. They also range into moist evergreen and deciduous forests, swamps, and multi-use plantation landscapes such as coffee, cardamom, and wattle estates that border protected forest — making conservation of these buffer areas as important as protecting core forest itself.

Diet

The Nilgiri Marten is an omnivore with a varied diet. It is known to prey on small mammals such as chevrotains and tree squirrels, as well as monitor lizards, crows, and cicadas, while also feeding on fruit. Its fruit-eating habit makes it a useful seed disperser, spreading seeds through the forest via its droppings.

Behaviour

Unusually for a marten, the species is primarily diurnal, active during daylight hours rather than at night. It is a cryptic, semi-arboreal animal, equally at home climbing trees and moving across the ground, and is known to maintain latrine sites where it deposits waste — likely a form of scent marking. Despite its striking colouring, it remains elusive and is rarely observed in the wild.

Reproduction

Very little is documented about the reproductive biology of the Nilgiri Marten. Its elusive, low-density, and cryptic nature in remote montane forest has made it difficult for researchers to study breeding behaviour, denning, or litter size in detail, and this remains a significant knowledge gap for the species.

Conservation Status

Vulnerable

The IUCN Red List classifies the Nilgiri Marten as Vulnerable (version 3.1), with an estimated population of only around 1,000 individuals remaining in the wild. It is also listed under Appendix III of CITES. The species qualifies as Vulnerable because its extent of occurrence is under 20,000 km², its range is severely fragmented, and the extent and quality of its forest habitat continues to decline.

Threats

  • Habitat loss and fragmentation across its already-limited Western Ghats range.
  • Continuing decline in the extent and quality of forest in and around the six discontinuous national parks where it occurs.
  • Conversion of forest fringes to tea, coffee, and timber plantations, which make up a large share of its known habitat niche.
  • Small, isolated population size, which increases vulnerability to local extinction.

Interesting Facts

  • It is the only marten species found anywhere in peninsular southern India — India's other marten, the Yellow-throated Marten, is a Himalayan species.
  • Most martens around the world are nocturnal, but the Nilgiri Marten is mainly active during the day.
  • It was only formally recorded in Wayanad for the first time in 2022, showing how much is still being learned about its range.
  • Its estimated wild population is only around 1,000 individuals.
  • It maintains latrine sites — shared toilet spots used for scent communication, a behaviour seen in several mustelid species.
