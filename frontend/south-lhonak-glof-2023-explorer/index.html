The 2023 South Lhonak GLOF

3–4 October 2023 · North Sikkim, Teesta basin

A moraine-dammed lake at about 5,200 m drained suddenly after a frozen slope collapsed into it, sending a debris-laden flood down the Teesta through Sikkim and West Bengal and onward toward Bangladesh.

Setting

Glacial lake geography

South Lhonak Lake sits in far northwestern Sikkim at 27.947°N, 88.332°E. It is a moraine-dammed lake fed by meltwater from the Lhonak Glacier — one of the fastest-growing glacial lakes in the Sikkim Himalaya.

How the lake grew

CORONA satellite images from 1962 show it as a small supraglacial pond on the glacier. By 1977 it was a separate lake of about 17 hectares. As the glacier retreated about 1.9 km over four decades, the lake reached nearly 100 hectares by 2008 and 167.4 hectares on 28 September 2023, days before the outburst.

Why it was already flagged

NRSC/ISRO listed it among Sikkim’s potentially dangerous glacial lakes as early as 2013. In 2018, pipelines were carried up by yak to siphon water and lower the lake. That work reduced volume but did not remove the outburst risk from a steep, frozen moraine dam.

Concept

What a GLOF is

A glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) is a sudden release of water stored in a lake dammed by ice or loose glacial debris (moraine). Failure can come from overtopping, dam collapse, or a displacement wave when ice, rock, or frozen slope material falls into the lake.

Once the dam is breached, the flood often erodes the valley floor, picks up sediment and boulders, and becomes a fast debris flow. Himalayan GLOF risk is rising as glaciers retreat and proglacial lakes grow behind unstable moraines.

What happened here

On 3 October 2023 at about 22:12 IST, roughly 14.7 million m³ of frozen lateral moraine collapsed into South Lhonak Lake. The impact raised a ~20 m displacement wave that overtopped and breached the frontal moraine. About 50 million m³ of water drained, with a reconstructed peak discharge near 48,500 m³/s.

Basin

Teesta River basin context

The outburst entered the steep Teesta catchment, where hydropower dams, highways, and towns sit on a narrow valley floor with little room to absorb a sudden surge.

The river

The Teesta forms in North Sikkim where the Lachen and Lachung rivers meet at Chungthang, then runs south through Mangan, Gangtok, Pakyong, and Namchi districts into the Darjeeling–Kalimpong hills of West Bengal, and on into Bangladesh to join the Brahmaputra. The 2023 flood travelled about 385 km along this path.

Why the valley amplified loss

Chungthang, Dikchu, Singtam, Rangpo, Melli, and Teesta Bazaar lie close to the channel. The 1,200 MW Teesta III dam at Chungthang stood about 50 km downstream of the lake. Heavy rain from a low-pressure system on 2–6 October added runoff after the outburst, so the GLOF, eroded sediment, secondary landslides, and rainfall acted together.

Chronology

Event timeline

  • 1962–2008: South Lhonak grows from a supraglacial pond to a large moraine-dammed lake as Lhonak Glacier retreats.
  • 2013: NRSC/ISRO hazard assessment flags the lake as having a high outburst probability.
  • 2018: Siphon pipes are installed to lower the lake level.
  • 6–18 September 2023: NDMA expeditions install solar-powered automated weather stations with cameras at South Lhonak and Shako Cho. This is a preparatory step toward a full early-warning system, not the completed system.
  • 19 September 2023: The South Lhonak station stops transmitting. ITBP inspects it on 28 September; the equipment is physically intact but cannot be revived.
  • 28 September 2023: Satellite imagery shows the lake at 167.4 hectares.
  • 3 October 2023, ~22:12 IST: Frozen lateral moraine collapses into the lake; the moraine dam is breached.
  • 4 October 2023, around midnight–01:00 IST: The flood reaches Chungthang. ITBP warns Teesta III operators; gates cannot be opened in time. The dam and the bridge to the powerhouse are destroyed within minutes.
  • 4 October, ~02:00 IST: Orders to open Dikchu dam gates come after floodwater has already arrived — showing no working downstream warning chain.
  • 4 October, 06:00 IST imagery: ISRO reports the lake reduced to 60.3 hectares — about 105 hectares drained.
Damage

Infrastructure impact

Dams, roads, and towns

Teesta III at Chungthang was destroyed. Other Teesta cascade dams were damaged. National Highway 10 failed in places, cutting North Sikkim and Gangtok from the rest of India. Fifteen to 31 major bridges were lost, depending on the count. Chungthang, Dikchu, Singtam, Rangpo, Melli, and Teesta Bazaar were among the hardest-hit settlements. About 3,000 tourists were stranded.

People and land

At least 92 people were confirmed dead by mid-October 2023; earlier situation reports listed 55 dead and dozens missing, including Indian Army personnel. Flooding reached Mangan, Gangtok, Pakyong, and Namchi in Sikkim, and Kalimpong, Darjeeling, Jalpaiguri, and Cooch Behar in West Bengal, then char lands along the Teesta in Bangladesh. A later scientific reconstruction estimated more than 25,900 buildings affected and about 270 km² of farmland inundated.

Detection

Early-warning challenges

The lake was a known high-risk site. What failed was last-mile warning: sensors that keep working at 5,200 m, and a chain of command that can open dams and move people in minutes, not hours.

  • The NDMA weather station at South Lhonak had already gone silent two weeks before the outburst.
  • Cameras and weather observations are not the same as an end-to-end early-warning system (lake sensors, river gauges, sirens, and notified operators).
  • ITBP’s midnight call reached Teesta III too late for the gates to open.
  • Downstream Dikchu received no usable warning before the water arrived.
  • Communities along the Teesta were largely outside the official risk conversation, which had stayed in scientific and bureaucratic circles.
Risk

Himalayan risk mitigation

India’s NDMA GLOF Risk Mitigation Programme aims to inventory high-risk lakes, install monitoring, and plan lake-lowering or downstream protection. South Lhonak showed that listing a lake and even siphoning it is not enough if the warning system is incomplete and valley floors are densely occupied by dams, highways, and towns.

Useful measures in the Himalaya combine lake management (controlled lowering, moraine inspection), rugged high-altitude sensors with backup, timed alerts to dam operators and villages, and land-use rules that keep new critical infrastructure out of mapped flood paths.

Practical points

  • Treat expanding moraine-dammed lakes as live hazards, not static scenery.
  • Design EWS for station failure: redundant sensors and human patrols (ITBP, local observers).
  • Rehearse dam-gate and evacuation timelines in minutes, not hours.
  • Map GLOF inundation for hydropower and NH corridors before new construction.
  • Tell downstream communities the risk in plain language, not only in technical reports.
