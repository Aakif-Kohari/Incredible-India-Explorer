Belum Caves — A Geological History

Carved by water that no longer flows

Beneath the flat, semi-arid plains of Kurnool district lies the second-largest cave system on the Indian subcontinent — a 3,229-metre labyrinth of passages, chambers, and freshwater galleries dissolved out of ancient limestone by a river that vanished long ago. Belum Caves are a textbook example of karst geology: what looks like solid rock from the surface hides tens of thousands of years of slow, patient underground erosion.

💧 Cave Formation

Belum Caves formed through karst dissolution — the slow chemical weathering of limestone by slightly acidic water. Over tens of thousands of years, an underground channel of the now-vanished Chitravathi River seeped through cracks in the limestone bedrock, gradually dissolving the softer rock while leaving the surrounding harder strata intact. This process carved out the long passages, spacious chambers, and freshwater galleries that define the cave system today. A small perennial stream, Patalaganga, still flows through the deepest reaches of the caves — flowing from southeast to northwest before disappearing, believed to re-emerge in a well at Belum village roughly 2 km away — a living remnant of the same water-driven process that built the entire cave system.

🪨 Limestone Geology

The caves are developed within the Narji Limestone, a formation belonging to the Kurnool Group of the larger Cuddapah Basin — one of India's most significant Proterozoic sedimentary basins. The wider Kurnool series includes shales, slates, limestones, sandstones, and quartzites, laid down as marine and near-shore sediments hundreds of millions of years ago, long before being uplifted and exposed at the surface. The specific limestone at Belum has a dark, near-black colouration, giving many of the cave's internal surfaces and formations their distinctive charcoal-grey tone.

A Classic Karst Terrain

Geologists studying the region have also documented dolines — large surface depressions formed when two or more underground caverns collapse and merge — a hallmark feature of mature karst landscapes, alongside the caves themselves.

🕳️ Underground Features

  • Gebauer Hall — the first major chamber encountered from the well-like entrance, named after German speleologist H. Daniel Gebauer, who led the 1982–83 mapping expedition.
  • Simhadwaram (Lion's Gate) — a natural stalactite arch resembling a lion's head, marking one of the cave's dramatic thresholds.
  • Kotilingalu Chamber — thousands of slender stalactite formations resembling Shiva lingams, alongside one massive column formed where a stalactite and stalagmite fused together.
  • Patalaganga — the deepest accessible point in the cave system, where the small perennial stream vanishes into the earth.
  • Saptasvarala Guha (Musical Chamber) — a chamber whose stalactite formations produce distinct musical tones when struck, a rare acoustic property tied to their internal mineral structure.
  • Thousand Hoods & Banyan Tree Hall — ceiling and pillar formations resembling cobra hoods and the aerial roots of a banyan tree, respectively.

🧊 Natural Formations (Speleothems)

Belum's most striking features are its speleothems — mineral deposits built up over millennia as dissolved limestone (calcium carbonate) precipitates back out of dripping water. Stalactites hang from cave ceilings where mineral-rich water drips slowly enough to leave deposits behind; stalagmites build upward from the floor beneath them, formed by the same dripping process. Where a stalactite and stalagmite grow long enough to meet, they fuse into a single floor-to-ceiling column — several dramatic examples of which are found throughout the cave system, most famously in the Kotilingalu Chamber.
