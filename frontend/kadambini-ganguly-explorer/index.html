Kadambini Ganguly

The Woman Who Broke Barriers in Indian Medicine (1861–1923)

Kadambini Ganguly was one of the first two women to graduate from the University of Calcutta — and from the entire British Empire — before becoming India's first practising woman physician of Western medicine. Defying social hostility at every step, she balanced a family of eight children with a pioneering medical career, spoke at the Indian National Congress, and forced institutions that had closed their doors to women to open them forever.

📚 Quick Facts

    Early Life

    A childhood shaped by the Brahmo Samaj's faith in women's education, in an age when girls were rarely taught to read.

    Kadambini Basu was born on 18 July 1861 in Bhagalpur, in the Bengal Presidency of British India (present-day Bihar). Her father, Brajakishore Basu, was a headmaster and a leading light of the Brahmo Samaj, the progressive reform movement founded under Rammohan Roy that championed education and equality for women. The family hailed from Chandsi village in Barisal district (now in Bangladesh).

    Education for girls was almost unheard of, but her father insisted his daughter study. Kadambini began at the Brahmo Eden Female School in Dacca and moved to Calcutta, joining the Hindu Mahila Vidyalaya, a pioneering "higher English school" for girls established in 1873 by the English educator Annette Ackroyd with the help of Brahmo reformers — among them Dwarakanath Ganguly, her future husband, who served as its headmaster. The school was renamed Banga Mahila Vidyalaya in 1876 and merged into Bethune School in 1878.

    In 1879 she passed the University of Calcutta's Entrance examination — by most accounts the first girl ever to do so — opening a door through which generations of Indian women would follow.

    Bethune College

    The classroom where two young women became the first female graduates of the British Empire.

    Admitted to the college classes of Bethune School — which grew into Bethune College — Kadambini studied alongside Chandramukhi Basu. In 1882 both passed the Bachelor of Arts examination of the University of Calcutta, and on 15 March 1883 they received their B.A. degrees at a convocation where the audience cheered the two young women loudly.

    They were the first female graduates not only of India but of the entire British Empire. Their success persuaded Bethune College to formally open its First Arts and degree courses to women, heralding a new era for women's higher education in India.

    On 12 June 1883, Kadambini married Dwarakanath Ganguly, a widower some twenty years her senior, editor of the women's journal Abala Bandhab ("Friend of the Weak"), and one of Bengal's foremost campaigners for women's emancipation. Their marriage, scandalous to polite society, became a true partnership: he encouraged her every step toward medicine.

    Medical Education

    The first woman to walk the corridors of Calcutta Medical College — and the resistance she faced there.

    Barely days after her wedding, Kadambini sought admission to the Calcutta Medical College, the oldest medical college in South Asia. The institution had never registered a woman student; professors objected, and the college had to change its age-old practice of admitting only men. Records variously date her registration to 1883 or 1884, but the fact is not disputed: she was its first woman student.

    In 1885 the government granted her a scholarship of twenty rupees a month. Even her final examination became a battleground: an examiner known for opposing women's medical education failed her by a single mark. The college nonetheless awarded her the special degree of GBMC (Graduate of Bengal Medical College) in 1886, qualifying her to practise medicine.

    That same year Anandibai Joshi, who had trained abroad at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania, also completed her degree — but died within months. It was Kadambini who actually practised, making her India's first practising woman physician of Western medicine.

    Medical Career

    From Lady Dufferin Hospital to a flourishing private practice — while raising eight children.

    In 1888 Kadambini took up an appointment at the Lady Dufferin Hospital on a salary of three hundred rupees a month, before building a successful private practice in Calcutta as a specialist in women's and children's health. She was simultaneously mother to eight children; contemporaries marvelled at how she balanced the wards and the nursery.

    Determined to deepen her skills against persistent whispers that her qualifications were inferior, she sailed for Britain in 1893. Within months she earned the celebrated "triple qualification" — licences from the medical corporations of Edinburgh, Glasgow and Dublin — becoming the first Indian woman to hold such credentials, at a time when Scottish colleges had only just begun admitting women at all.

    “The hostility she met was open and organised. A Bengali periodical, Bangabashi, vilified her as unworthy of her profession; her husband sued its editor for libel and won, sending him to jail. Mahatma Gandhi himself once criticised her attire in Indian Opinion — and later withdrew the remark with an apology.”

    Public Life

    A voice for women inside the Indian National Congress and beyond.

    At the 1889 Annual Session of the Indian National Congress in Bombay, Kadambini Ganguly stood among the handful of women delegates in the hall — and moved the vote of thanks, becoming one of the first women ever to address the Congress from its platform. Annie Besant hailed her as "a symbol that India's freedom will uplift India's womanhood."

    She campaigned for women's education and for the training of women teachers, joined protests during the Swadeshi movement after the 1905 partition of Bengal, and continued to press for women's entry into the professions. In 1915, addressing a medical conference at Calcutta Medical College — the college that had once shut its doors against her — she argued so compellingly that the institution revised its policies and opened its classes to female students.
