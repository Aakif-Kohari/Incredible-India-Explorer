Introduction

The Brown Palm Civet (Paradoxurus jerdoni), also known as Jerdon's Palm Civet, is a small, nocturnal, tree-dwelling carnivore found nowhere on Earth except the rainforests of the Western Ghats of India. Elusive and rarely seen, it plays an outsized role in its ecosystem as a prolific seed disperser, quietly shaping the rainforests it calls home.

Scientific Name

Paradoxurus jerdoni Blanford, 1885. The species was first described by British naturalist William Thomas Blanford in 1885, from a skull and pelt collected in Kodaikanal, Tamil Nadu. The specific epithet jerdoni honours Thomas Caverhill Jerdon, a 19th-century naturalist known for his work on Indian fauna. The genus name Paradoxurus comes from the Greek for "strange tail."

Physical Characteristics

  • Uniformly brown pelage, ranging from pale buff to dark brown, darker around the head, neck, shoulders, legs, and tail.
  • No distinct facial markings, unlike the related Asian Palm Civet.
  • A distinctive reversed direction of hair growth on the nape — a trait shared only with the Golden Palm Civet of Sri Lanka.
  • Tail is long and sleek, sometimes tipped white or pale yellow.
  • Head-and-body length: roughly 430–620 mm; tail length: roughly 380–530 mm.
  • Adult body weight: approximately 1.5–4.3 kg.

Distribution Map

The Brown Palm Civet is endemic to the Western Ghats mountain range, ranging along the hill forests from Castle Rock in Goa in the north to the Kalakkad–Mundanthurai Tiger Reserve at the southern tip of the range.

States: Goa, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu.

Habitat

Brown Palm Civets thrive in moist evergreen and semi-evergreen tropical rainforest canopy, typically at elevations between 500 and 1,300 metres, although sightings as low as 78 metres have been recorded. Being arboreal, they spend most of their lives in the forest canopy and rarely descend to the ground, navigating fragmented landscapes that include coffee, tea, and cardamom plantations bordering natural forest.

Diet

The species is predominantly frugivorous. A long-term study analysing over 1,000 scat samples in the Kalakkad–Mundanthurai Tiger Reserve found that fruit made up around 97% of the diet, drawn from more than 50 native plant species. The remainder is supplemented with invertebrates and occasional small vertebrates, making the civet an opportunistic omnivore with a strong fruit preference.

Behaviour

Brown Palm Civets are solitary and strictly nocturnal. During the day, they rest in day-bed sites such as tree hollows, dense canopy vine tangles, or abandoned squirrel nests, emerging after dusk to forage in the canopy. Their elusive, quiet nature and nocturnal habits make direct sightings rare, and much of what is known about the species comes from camera-trap surveys and scat analysis rather than direct observation.

Reproduction

Detailed reproductive data for the Brown Palm Civet remains limited, owing to its elusive, nocturnal, and canopy-dwelling lifestyle. As with other Paradoxurus civets, litters are generally small. Field research on breeding behaviour, denning sites, and life history is still an active and ongoing area of study.

Ecological Importance

As a highly frugivorous mammal, the Brown Palm Civet is a key seed disperser in the Western Ghats rainforest. By consuming fruit and excreting viable seeds away from the parent tree, it helps maintain plant diversity, supports forest regeneration, and contributes to the long-term health and connectivity of the rainforest ecosystem it inhabits.

Conservation Status (IUCN)

Least Concern

The IUCN Red List classifies the Brown Palm Civet as Least Concern (version 3.1). It is also listed under Appendix III of CITES and Schedule II of the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, which affords it legal protection within India.

Threats

  • Habitat fragmentation from coffee, tea, and cardamom plantations breaking up continuous forest cover.
  • Deforestation and conversion of rainforest for agriculture and infrastructure.
  • Hunting in parts of its range, where it is taken for meat and for fat used in traditional medicine.
  • Roadkill and disturbance linked to expanding roads and human settlement near forest edges.

Conservation Efforts

  • Legal protection under the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (Schedule II).
  • Presence within a network of Western Ghats tiger reserves, national parks, and wildlife sanctuaries.
  • Ongoing ecological research — including scat analysis and camera-trap and habitat-suitability studies — to better understand distribution and habitat needs.
  • Habitat corridor and plantation-landscape conservation efforts to reconnect fragmented rainforest patches.

Interesting Facts

  • It is named after Thomas Caverhill Jerdon, the 19th-century naturalist who authored "The Mammals of India."
  • The reversed hair growth on its nape is a trait shared only with the Golden Palm Civet of Sri Lanka.
  • Of the three Paradoxurus civet species, it is the only one entirely endemic to the Western Ghats.
  • Its highly frugivorous diet has earned it a reputation as a "gardener of the forest" for the seeds it disperses.
  • A 2021 sighting in Kerala's Arippa forest, at just 78 m elevation, was unusually low for the species.
