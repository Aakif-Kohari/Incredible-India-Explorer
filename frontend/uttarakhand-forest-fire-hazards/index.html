Forest Fire Hazards of Uttarakhand

Where resin-rich pine meets a warming, drying Himalaya

Uttarakhand holds just 1.6% of India's land area but 45% forest cover — more than double the national average. Each dry season, its resin-laden chir pine belt turns that green wealth into fuel. What was once a manageable, community-managed seasonal risk has, in recent years, become one of the most severe recurring forest-fire crises in the Indian Himalaya.

🌲 Forest Geography

Uttarakhand's forests span montane, sub-alpine, and alpine ecosystems across a steep elevation gradient. In the foothills and Tarai plains, moist mixed broadleaf and Sal (Shorea robusta) forests dominate. Between roughly 1,000 and 1,800 metres, chir pine (Pinus roxburghii) covers approximately 26–28% of the state's forest area, often mixed with or replacing banj oak (Quercus leucotrichophora). Higher still, deodar, blue pine (kail), fir, and spruce take over. Above roughly 2,500 metres, fires are rare — the forests are moister and human settlement thins out. It is the mid-elevation chir pine belt that carries almost all of the state's fire risk.

📅 Fire-Prone Seasons

The traditional fire season runs from February through mid-June, peaking in April and May as dry needle litter accumulates and pre-monsoon heat sets in. The 2023–24 season was especially severe: the Uttarakhand Forest Department recorded 11,256 fire incidents across 11 of the state's 13 districts between November 2023 and June 2024 — with 2024 alone claiming 12 lives, the highest annual toll on record, including six forest department personnel.

A Shifting Season

In recent years, fires have begun appearing even in winter months, well outside the traditional window — a pattern researchers link to reduced pre-monsoon rainfall and rising temperatures, effectively lengthening the fire-risk period each year.

🔥 Natural and Human Contributing Factors

  • Resin-rich chir pine needles — the dry litter is highly flammable and colonial-era forestry policy favoured pine for its commercial timber and turpentine value, letting it spread at the expense of fire-resistant broadleaf species like oak and sal.
  • Legal protection that backfires — tree conservation rules restrict cutting chir pine above 1,000 m, unintentionally allowing it to keep encroaching into neighbouring oak and sal forests.
  • Human ignition — a large share of fires are traced to human activity: agricultural burning, careless discarding of cigarettes or matches, and deliberate clearing of undergrowth. One 2024 bulletin logged 351 fires as confirmed human-caused, with dozens of suspects identified.
  • Pre-monsoon drought and heat — low humidity and rising temperatures dry out the forest floor ahead of peak fire months.
  • Steep, hard-to-access terrain — the mountainous topography of Kumaon and Garhwal makes both suppression and evacuation far harder than on flat ground.
  • Under-resourced firefighting capacity — a few thousand forest guards, some with unpaid salaries and inadequate equipment, are responsible for managing fires across lakhs of hectares.

🌍 Ecological Impacts

Repeated burning clears the forest floor of broadleaf species, creating conditions that favour even more chir pine regeneration — a self-reinforcing cycle that gradually pushes out oak and sal. Research using Landsat and MODIS satellite data shows Sal forests are relatively resilient to low-intensity fires, while Pine forests are far more sensitive, losing significant carbon stock with each fire cycle. Beyond vegetation loss, fires damage soil structure, increase erosion risk on already steep slopes, and disrupt watershed function in a state that feeds several major Himalayan rivers.

💨 Smoke and Air-Quality Effects

Large fire events measurably alter the atmosphere over the region. During the major 2016 fire event, carbon monoxide levels observed over Dehradun rose sharply, peaking at over 700 ppbv — alongside elevated ozone and nitrogen oxides. Smoke and ash also carry black carbon long distances, where it settles on Himalayan glaciers.

A 2016 Wadia Institute of Himalayan Geology study at the Chirbasa station near Gangotri glacier found black carbon levels in summer were nearly 400 times higher than in winter — accelerating glacial melt by darkening the ice surface and reducing its reflectivity (albedo).

From Forest Fire to Flood Risk

Scientists warn this creates a downstream hazard chain: faster glacier melt from black-carbon deposition can increase the risk of glacial lake outburst floods and water shortages in the very river systems Uttarakhand's forests are meant to help regulate.

🛰️ Fire Monitoring

The Forest Survey of India (FSI) tracks active fires nationwide using satellite hotspot detection from MODIS and SNPP-VIIRS sensors, issuing near-real-time alerts to state forest departments. During peak-season weeks, FSI data has repeatedly shown Uttarakhand among the states with the highest concentration of active fire points in the country. On the ground, the state Forest Department publishes daily fire bulletins by division (Garhwal vs. Kumaon) and district, and has deployed resources including the Indian Air Force and NDRF during severe outbreaks, such as the major 2016 season.

🛡️ Prevention Measures

  • Pine-needle (pirul) management — organised collection of fallen pine needles, both to remove fuel load and to supply small-scale briquetting and biomass industries that create local livelihoods.
  • Fire-line maintenance — clearing and maintaining containment lines ('beats') that were originally established during the colonial era, which local advocates argue need renewed funding.
  • Broadleaf restoration — planting oak, rhododendron, and other fire-resistant native species to gradually reduce the dominance of flammable pine monocultures.
  • Joint Forest Management (JFM) — structured collaboration between the Forest Department and local communities on prevention planning.
  • Public awareness campaigns — using radio, television, and social media to spread fire-season advisories and safe-burning practices.

🤝 Community Participation: The Van Panchayat Model

Uttarakhand's forests have a distinctive institution: the Van Panchayat, a village-level forest council with legal rights to manage designated community forest land — a system unique among Indian states. Historically, when a fire broke out, one village would send people to douse it with green-leaf brooms while another brought water and food; managing fires was treated as a shared community responsibility.

Environmentalists argue this local knowledge and mobilisation capacity has weakened over decades and needs deliberate strengthening — since a few thousand paid forest guards cannot realistically patrol and respond across lakhs of hectares of steep terrain alone. In the aftermath of the severe 2024 fire season, several community-led initiatives — including women-led village groups adopting early-warning tools and coordinated patrols — have re-emerged as a frontline response, working alongside the Forest Department rather than replacing it.

💡 Key Facts

  • Uttarakhand covers just 1.6% of India's land area but holds 45% forest cover, versus a 21.67% national average.
  • Chir pine covers roughly 26–28% of the state's total forest area.
  • The severe 2016 fire season recorded 2,069 incidents affecting 4,423 hectares, prompting NDRF and helicopter deployment.
  • Between November 2020 and June 2021, Uttarakhand alone reported 21,487 forest fires.
  • About 36% of the state's forest area is classified as vulnerable to frequent fires, with 7.85% highly sensitive (India State Forest Report).
  • Fires occur mainly in the Garhwal division (Uttarkashi, Pauri, Rudraprayag, Chamoli, Dehradun) and Kumaon division (Almora, Bageshwar, Pithoragarh, Nainital, Udham Singh Nagar).
