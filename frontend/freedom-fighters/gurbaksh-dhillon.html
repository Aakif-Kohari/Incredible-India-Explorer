Indian National Army (INA) · Azad Hind Fauj

Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon

A courageous patriot and commander of the Nehru Brigade, whose historic trial at the Red Fort ignited a unified national uproar that shook the foundations of the British Empire in India.

🎖️ Quick Facts

Organization: Indian National Army (INA)
Rank/Role: Colonel, Commander of the Nehru Brigade
Key Theater: Burma Campaign (Irrawaddy River)
Historic Event: The Red Fort Trials (1945-1946)
Legacy: Padma Bhushan (1998)
From British Officer to Patriot

Early Life & Military Background

Born on March 18, 1914, in Algon, Punjab, Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon initially joined the British Indian Army, serving in the 1/14th Punjab Regiment. Like many Indian soldiers of his generation, his trajectory changed dramatically during World War II when he was deployed to the Malayan campaign.

Following the Fall of Singapore in 1942, Dhillon was among the thousands of Indian prisoners of war taken by the Japanese. Inspired by the fervent patriotism of Mohan Singh and later Subhas Chandra Bose (Netaji), Dhillon made the pivotal decision to switch his allegiance from the British Crown to the cause of Indian independence, joining the Azad Hind Fauj (INA).

🇮🇳 The Call of Azad Hind

Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose's famous rallying cry, "Chalo Delhi!" (Onward to Delhi), deeply resonated with Dhillon. He committed himself to an armed struggle to liberate India from colonial rule, regardless of the personal cost.

The Battlefield

Commanding the Nehru Brigade

The Nehru Brigade

Dhillon was appointed as the commander of the 4th Guerrilla Regiment, famously known as the Nehru Brigade, demonstrating extreme tactical resilience under harsh conditions.

Irrawaddy River Defense

In early 1945, Dhillon's forces were tasked with holding the Irrawaddy River line in Burma against advancing British Allied forces, fighting bravely despite severe shortages of rations and ammunition.

Capture

Overwhelmed by superior Allied firepower and logistics, INA forces were eventually pushed back. Dhillon, alongside thousands of his comrades, was captured in Pegu, Burma, and brought back to India as a prisoner.

⚖️ A Symbol of Unity

The British decision to jointly try a Sikh (Dhillon), a Hindu (Prem Kumar Sahgal), and a Muslim (Shah Nawaz Khan) backfired spectacularly, forging unprecedented inter-communal solidarity across India in defense of the "patriot-traitors."

The Landmark Legal Battle

The Red Fort Trials (1945–1946)

The British colonial government decided to publicly court-martial Dhillon, along with Major General Shah Nawaz Khan and Colonel Prem Kumar Sahgal, at the historic Red Fort in Delhi. They were charged with "waging war against the King-Emperor."

Instead of acting as a deterrent, the trial ignited a massive nationwide outcry. The Indian National Congress formed the INA Defence Committee, comprising legal stalwarts like Bhulabhai Desai, Tej Bahadur Sapru, and Jawaharlal Nehru. Although the three officers were found guilty, the overwhelming public pressure and the threat of widespread mutiny within the British Indian Armed Forces forced the Commander-in-Chief, Claude Auchinleck, to remit their sentences. They were released to a hero's welcome.

A Nation's Gratitude

Legacy & References

Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon’s legacy is inextricably linked to the final, decisive blow against British rule in India. The INA trials exposed the vulnerability of the British hold on the Indian military, directly contributing to the Royal Indian Navy mutiny of 1946 and accelerating the timeline for Indian independence. His life remains a profound testament to duty, sacrifice, and secular unity.
