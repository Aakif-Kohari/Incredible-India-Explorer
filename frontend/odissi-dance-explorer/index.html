01 — Origin & History

From royal court to temple sanctum

Odissi's story is usually traced to the Udayagiri and Khandagiri caves near Bhubaneswar. A carved panel in the Ranigumpha (Queen's Cave) shows dancers and musicians performing before a royal audience, and a nearby inscription in the Hathigumpha cave records a dance performance staged for King Kharavela — evidence that dance had a place in Odisha's court culture as early as the 2nd century BCE, long before it became a temple ritual.

The dance's deeper attachment to religion grew from the 6th century onward, as rulers built major temples across Bhubaneswar, and reached its height with the 12th-century Jagannath Temple at Puri and the 13th-century Konark Sun Temple. Inside the Jagannath Temple, a class of temple dancers called Maharis (also known as devadasis) performed daily as a form of worship — dancing during the morning food offering and singing before the deity was put to rest at night. A parallel, more acrobatic tradition called Gotipua emerged, in which young boys dressed as girls trained to perform both inside temples and at festivals and fairs, sustaining the technique when the mahari system eventually declined.

Colonial-era social reform campaigns against temple dancing pushed Odissi to the margins by the early 20th century. Its revival after Indian independence was a reconstruction project as much as a performance one: gurus studied the dance poses frozen in temple sculpture and cross-referenced them with treatises like the Natya Shastra, Abhinaya Chandrika and Abhinaya Darpana to rebuild a coherent classical technique, which was formally recognised as one of India's classical dance forms in the 1950s–60s.

02 — Dance Characteristics

Sculpture, in motion

Odissi is often called a "mobile sculpture" because its stances echo the carved figures on Odisha's temples. It balances two opposite energies — vigorous tandava and graceful lasya — through independent, layered movement of the head, chest and hips, a torso technique that sets it apart from other Indian classical styles.

卐

Tandava & Lasya

The dance fuses Shiva's forceful, masculine tandava with Krishna's soft, lyrical lasya — strength and grace held in the same body.

◧

Chowka

A square, grounded stance with bent knees and outstretched arms, said to symbolise Lord Jagannath and representing stability and strength.

S

Tribhangi

The "three-bend" S-curve of head, torso and knee, associated with Krishna — Odissi's most recognisable and graceful posture.

✋

Hasta Mudras

Codified hand gestures drawn from the Natya Shastra narrate stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata and Jayadeva's Gita Govinda.

A traditional recital, in five movements

I
Mangalacharan

An invocation to Jagannath and Ganesh, plus apologies to the earth and audience for the dance about to follow.

II
Batu / Sthai

Pure, abstract dance (nritta) built on Chowka and Tribhangi, with no narrative content — technique for its own sake.

III
Pallavi

A melodic expansion of pure dance, elaborating a raga through increasingly intricate rhythm and movement.

IV
Abhinaya

Expressive storytelling (nritya), often set to verses from Jayadeva's Gita Govinda, using facial expression and mudra.

V
Moksha

A closing pure-dance sequence symbolising spiritual liberation, ending the recital on a note of release.

04 — Musical Instruments

The Odissi orchestra

Odissi music blends northern and southern Indian traditions of raga and tala, giving the dance its distinctive melodic and rhythmic base.

🥁
Mardala

A barrel-shaped drum and the dance's principal rhythmic voice, closely tied to its footwork.

🎼
Bansuri (Flute)

Carries the melodic line, evoking Krishna, who is traditionally depicted playing the flute.

🎻
Violin

Adopted into the ensemble to double or ornament the vocal and melodic line.

🔔
Manjira

Small hand cymbals that mark the tala, the rhythmic cycle underlying each piece.

🎹
Harmonium

Provides melodic and drone support for the vocal compositions sung during abhinaya.

07 — Dance Timeline

Two thousand years, condensed

2nd c. BCE
Court dance at Udayagiri

Cave carvings and King Kharavela's Hathigumpha inscription record dance performed at the royal court.

6th–13th c. CE
Temple-building era

Bhubaneswar, Puri and Konark temples are built; their sculpture panels become a visual record of dance postures.

12th c.
Mahari tradition formalised

The Jagannath Temple institutionalises daily ritual dance by temple dancers known as maharis.

Medieval era
Gotipua tradition

Boys trained as girl-dancers perform outside temple sanctums, keeping technique alive as mahari numbers decline.

Colonial period
Suppression

Anti-nautch reform movements curb temple dancing; the tradition survives on the margins.

1950s–60s
Revival & codification

Gurus reconstruct technique from temple sculpture and dance treatises; Odissi is recognised as a classical form.

Today
A global classical art

Odissi is taught and performed worldwide, one of eight forms recognised by India's Sangeet Natak Akademi.

08 — Major Performance Traditions

Who carried the dance forward

Mahari tradition

Temple dancers dedicated to Jagannath's service, performing inside the sanctum as a daily act of worship rather than public entertainment — Odissi's original, sacred context.

Gotipua tradition

Young boys, dressed and trained as girls, performed acrobatic, athletic Odissi at temples and festivals — still practised today in villages such as Raghurajpur.

Post-independence revival

20th-century gurus rebuilt Odissi as a codified classical form for the concert stage, drawing directly on temple sculpture to recover lost technique.

Odissi today

Now performed and taught internationally, Odissi continues to draw on Jayadeva's Gita Govinda and Odia literature while reaching new audiences worldwide.
