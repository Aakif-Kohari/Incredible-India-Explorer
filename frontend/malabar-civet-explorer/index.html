Malabar Civet (Viverra civettina)

The Malabar Large-spotted Civet is one of the world's most elusive, critically endangered mammals. Endemic to the lowland moist forests and coastal thickets of the Western Ghats, it remains a symbol of urgent wilderness conservation.

Overview

India's Elusive Phantom

The Malabar Large-spotted Civet (Viverra civettina), known locally as Kannan Changu in Malayalam, is a viverrid endemic to the Western Ghats of South India. Once feared extinct during the mid-20th century, a few skins and isolated specimens were rediscovered in the late 1980s.

With an estimated wild population of fewer than 250 mature individuals, it ranks among the top 15 most threatened mammals worldwide.

Key Highlights

  • Distinct Markings: Dusky grey coat featuring large dark spots on flanks and transverse neck stripes.
  • Nocturnal & Solitary: Strictly nocturnal, active only under total darkness in dense cover.
  • Lowland Specialist: Restricted to coastal plains and low-elevation moist evergreen thickets.
  • Legal Protection: Schedule I under India's Wildlife Protection Act.

Distribution

Historic & Present Range

Historically ranged along the coastal moist evergreen forests and foothills of the Western Ghats from Kanyakumari up to Honnavar in Karnataka.

Presently restricted to tiny, fragmented remnants in northern Kerala (Kuttyadi, Koduvally, Nileshwar) and southern Karnataka.

Estimated Population

< 250

Extremely low wild population density with fragmented, isolated breeding units.

Habitat

Unlike montane Western Ghats species, the Malabar Civet is specialized for lowland ecosystems below 600 metres elevation.

Lowland Moist Evergreen Forests

Dense understorey and thick leaf-littered floors in humid coastal river valleys.

Secondary Thickets & Cashew Plantations

Overgrown cashew groves providing cover and fallen fruit foraging grounds.

Coastal Swamp Strips

Riparian and wetland-margin scrublands rich in small aquatic prey.

Behaviour

Nocturnal & Terrestrial Lifestyle

Unlike many tree-dwelling civets, the Malabar Civet is primarily terrestrial. It rests during the day in dense ground brush or hollow burrows and emerges strictly after dark to forage.

Solitary & Scent Marking

Strictly solitary animals that defend exclusive home territories using musk secretions produced by perineal civet glands.

Diet

Omnivorous feeder that hunts small animals and consumes fallen fruits:

Threats

Lowland Coastal Habitat Loss

Lowland forests along the Malabar Coast have been almost completely cleared for agriculture, human settlements, and roads.

Accidental Trapping & Hunting

Caught in indiscriminate snares set for small game or hunted for its pelt and civet musk secretion.

Predation by Feral Dogs

Ground-dwelling habits near human margins expose them to attacks by free-ranging domestic dogs.

Conservation Measures

Camera Trap Surveys

Targeted camera trap networks across cashew plantations and forest patches to locate surviving individuals.

Habitat Corridor Protection

Protecting remnant cashew groves and private scrublands that act as vital movement corridors.

Community Awareness

Engaging local farmers and plantations to prevent snaring and report sightings.

Interesting Facts

01

Thought Extinct: Presumed extinct for over 30 years until skin specimens were found in 1987 in Kerala markets.

02

Crested Mane: Possesses a erectile crest of black hair along its spine that raises when alarmed.

03

Cashew Alliance: Adapted to forage in introduced cashew plantations, which now serve as critical artificial refuges.

04

Civetone Scent: Secretes a strong musk compound (civetone) used historically in perfume manufacturing.
