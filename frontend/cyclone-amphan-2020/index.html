Cyclone Amphan: The Super Cyclone That Tested Eastern India

Bay of Bengal · May 2020 · The Costliest Cyclone Recorded in the North Indian Ocean

In May 2020, Super Cyclonic Storm Amphan (pronounced Um-pun) grew over the warm waters of the Bay of Bengal into the region's most powerful storm since the 1999 Odisha Super Cyclone. Although it weakened before landfall, Amphan slammed into the West Bengal coast near Bakkhali on 20 May 2020 with winds gusting to about 185 km/h, drove a 3–5 metre storm surge through the Sundarbans, flooded Kolkata, and caused damage exceeding US$13 billion — even as India battled the COVID-19 pandemic.

🌀 Cyclone Formation: From Andaman Sea to Monster

Around 13 May 2020, a low-pressure area consolidated over the Andaman Sea and southeast Bay of Bengal. Conditions were close to ideal for cyclogenesis: sea-surface temperatures of 30–32°C, abundant moisture, weak vertical wind shear, and strong upper-level outflow. By 16 May the system had organised into a depression, and on 17 May the India Meteorological Department (IMD) named it Amphan — a name contributed by Thailand meaning "sky".

Over the following 36 hours the storm underwent rapid intensification, exploding from a cyclonic storm into an Extremely Severe Cyclonic Storm and then — on 18 May — into a Super Cyclonic Storm with sustained winds of roughly 240 km/h (gusts near 265 km/h), equivalent to Category 5 on the Saffir–Simpson scale. It was the first super cyclonic storm in the Bay of Bengal since the catastrophic 1999 Odisha Super Cyclone.

  • Warm Ocean Fuel: Pre-monsoon Bay of Bengal waters above 30°C provided enormous heat and moisture energy.
  • Rapid Intensification: Low wind shear allowed the eye to contract and the eyewall to intensify dramatically within two days.
  • Weakening Before Landfall: Cooler upwelled water, an eyewall replacement cycle and interaction with land reduced Amphan to a Very Severe Cyclonic Storm (~155–165 km/h) before it struck the coast.

⏱️ Track & Timeline: Eight Days That Shook the East Coast

Amphan followed a classic pre-monsoon Bay of Bengal track — steered northwest across the open ocean, then recurving northeast toward the Bengal–Bangladesh delta coast.

  1. 13–15 May Low-pressure area forms over the Andaman Sea and drifts northwest into the Bay of Bengal.
  2. 16 May System consolidates into a depression, then a deep depression, tracking north-northwest.
  3. 17 May Named Cyclonic Storm Amphan; rapid intensification follows — very severe, then extremely severe, within hours.
  4. 18 May Peaks as a Super Cyclonic Storm (~240 km/h) over the west-central Bay of Bengal.
  5. 19 May Weakened but still dangerous, it brushes past the Odisha coast; heavy rain begins along the shoreline.
  6. 20 May (afternoon) Landfall between Digha (West Bengal) and Hatiya Island (Bangladesh), close to Sagar Island and Bakkhali, at ~2:30 PM IST with sustained winds of 155–165 km/h. The core crosses Kolkata after dark.
  7. 20–21 May The storm sweeps north-northeast through Bangladesh and Northeast India, unleashing torrential rain and flooding rivers.
  8. 21 May Weakens into a cyclonic storm over Bangladesh (centred near 24.7°N, 89.5°E by dawn) and degenerates into a depression the same day.

🇮🇳 Indian Impact: West Bengal & Odisha

Amphan affected more than 18 million people across eastern India and Bangladesh and claimed over a hundred lives in India — the vast majority in West Bengal. West Bengal Chief Minister Mamata Banerjee described it as an ordeal "bigger than COVID-19", estimating state losses of about ₹1 lakh crore (US$13+ billion).

Kolkata: A Metropolis Paralysed

The storm core passed directly over the Kolkata metropolitan area after nightfall. Ferocious gusts uprooted thousands of trees, snapped power lines and crushed vehicles and homes. The city suffered a near-total electricity blackout lasting days, water supply was disrupted, streets became canals of debris, and Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose International Airport saw its hangar damaged and operations suspended.

Sundarbans & South 24 Parganas

The landfall zone took the worst hit. Mud embankments protecting dozens of delta villages were breached, letting saline surge water flood homes, ponds and farmland. Thousands of mud-and-thatch houses collapsed, fisheries and poultry were wiped out, and families sheltering inside were rescued by boat.

Districts Across South Bengal

Purba Medinipur (Contai, Digha belt), Paschim Medinipur, Howrah, Hooghly, North 24 Parganas and parts of Nadia and Murshidabad reported destroyed standing harvests, flattened vegetable fields, shattered mango orchards, and ruined jute crops — striking at the heart of Bengal's rural economy during harvest season.

Odisha: Brushed by the Northern Edge

Amphan's circulation swept across coastal Odisha — Balasore, Bhadrak, Kendrapara, Jagatsinghpur and Paradip belt — bringing heavy rain, gale winds and localized sea-water ingress into low-lying villages. Thanks to massive pre-landfall evacuations and post-Fani readiness, casualties remained minimal, though crops, roads and electricity infrastructure suffered damage.

🌊 Storm Surge, Flooding & Coastal Hazards Explained

Wind is only one weapon of a tropical cyclone. Along low-lying deltas, water does most of the killing and destroying:

🌊
Storm Surge (3–5 m): The storm's winds and low pressure piled sea water up to 4–5 metres above the astronomical tide onto the West Bengal coast — inundating villages across the Sundarbans and Contai–Digha belt, exactly as IMD had warned before landfall.
⏱️
Tidal Timing (Compound Hazard): Amphan struck around high spring tides, so surge, tide and river discharge combined — pushing brackish water kilometres inland through river mouths and breached embankments.
🌧️
Inland Flooding: Torrential rainfall of 100–300 mm+ swamped Kolkata and neighbouring districts, while the same rain bands triggered floods and landslides in Bangladesh, Meghalaya and Assam.
🧂
Salinisation: Surge water laced with salt poisoned paddy fields, ponds and drinking-water sources for years to come — a slow-motion agricultural disaster long after floodwaters receded.
🌪️
Wind Damage: Gale winds flattening weak structures, snapping electricity poles and felling urban trees remain the signature hazard for cities like Kolkata built beneath ageing canopies.

🚨 Evacuation & Emergency Response

Even amid the COVID-19 pandemic — which forced authorities to run shelters at reduced capacity with masks and distancing — India executed one of history's largest peacetime evacuations. Close to five million people were moved to safety in West Bengal and Odisha combined, alongside millions more across the border in Bangladesh. This mass pre-emptive evacuation, guided by accurate IMD forecasts five days ahead, is credited with keeping the death toll remarkably low relative to the storm's ferocity — especially when compared with the thousands killed by comparably intense storms in previous decades, such as the 1999 Odisha Super Cyclone.

📡 Forecast & Warning

IMD tracked Amphan continuously via satellites, radar and buoy data, issuing cyclone alerts for Odisha and West Bengal coasts well in advance of landfall.

🛖 Mass Evacuation

Nearly 5 million people moved to cyclone shelters, schools and pucca buildings in West Bengal and Odisha; fishermen warned ashore and ports placed on alert.

🧑‍🚒 Field Deployment

Over 20 NDRF teams positioned with boats and rescue gear, backed by state police, fire services, army columns and air force assets on standby.

🏥 COVID-Safe Shelters

Masks, sanitizers and physical spacing arranged inside shelters — a first-of-its-kind dual-disaster operation for eastern India.

💰 Relief Announcements

The Prime Minister announced ₹1,000 crore immediate assistance for West Bengal; ex-gratia payments were made to families of the deceased and injured.

🔧 Restoration

Armed forces helped clear tree-blocked roads; power utilities, telecom operators and civic bodies worked around the clock for days to restore essential services in Kolkata.

🌿 Mangroves: The Sundarbans' Natural Shield

Amphan was a live test of how well nature defends the coast. The Sundarbans mangrove belt — the world's largest mangrove forest and a UNESCO World Heritage site shared with Bangladesh — absorbed part of the surge energy before it reached interior villages. Dense mangrove roots and trunks reduce wave height and surge velocity substantially, protecting settlements behind them.

Yet decades of embankment-dependent settlement, mangrove clearing and rising sea levels have thinned this shield. After Amphan, restoration became central to recovery policy:

  • Embankment Reality Check: Hundreds of earthen embankment breaches showed that concrete-only defences cannot replace living shorelines.
  • Mangrove Restoration: Large-scale replanting programmes were accelerated along seaward faces to rebuild the bio-shield.
  • Community Protection: Village-level committees now combine plantation drives with embankment repair and early-warning drills.
  • Climate Signal: Warming seas are intensifying Bay of Bengal cyclones, making natural coastal buffers a matter of survival for the delta's millions.

📚 Lessons Learned from Amphan

  1. Warning Works Accurate five-day forecasts and mass evacuation saved countless lives — the single biggest reason fatalities stayed in the hundreds rather than the thousands.
  2. Harden Cities Kolkata's blackout exposed fragile grids: underground cabling, tree management and flood-proof substations are now planning priorities.
  3. Plan for Compound Disasters COVID-19 + cyclone proved agencies must rehearse simultaneous crises — shelters, logistics and communication must work under pandemic rules too.
  4. Invest in Nature Mangrove belts and healthy deltas blunt surges better than embankments alone; green-grey hybrid defence is the future.
  5. Protect Livelihoods Crop insurance, saline-tolerant seeds and fishery support speed recovery for farming and fishing communities hit by salinisation.
  6. Build Back Safer Reconstruction adopted stronger housing designs, raised plinths and improved multi-purpose cyclone shelters across the affected districts.

🧭 Explorer Hub: Fast Facts & Sources

  • Name Origin: Amphan (pronounced "Um-pun") is a Thai word for "sky", contributed by Thailand to the WMO/ESCAP cyclone naming list.
  • Historic Peak: First Super Cyclonic Storm in the Bay of Bengal since the 1999 Odisha Super Cyclone.
  • Record Cost: Damage estimates of ~US$13 billion+ made Amphan the costliest cyclone ever recorded in the North Indian Ocean.
  • Dual Crisis: The entire evacuation and relief effort ran under COVID-19 lockdown restrictions — masks inside cyclone shelters became a defining image of 2020.
  • Wildlife Echo: After the surge, a Royal Bengal Tiger from the flooded Sundarbans was found sheltering in a village home near Gosaba — nature and people sharing the same refuge.
