Handloom — India's Living Textile Heritage

From the pit looms of Varanasi to the tie-dyed streets of Kutch, handloom weaving is one of India's oldest continuous crafts — a inheritance carried thread by thread through millions of artisan hands, second only to agriculture as the country's largest source of rural livelihood.

Craft

Weaving techniques

Handloom is a family of techniques, not one method — each requiring years to master and often dividing labour across several artisans.

Pit-loom weaving

The weaver sits with feet in a dug pit, operating pedals that lift warp threads while a shuttle carries the weft across by hand — the base method behind Banarasi and Kanjivaram silks.

Ikat (resist-dyeing yarn)

Bundles of yarn are tied and dyed in sections before weaving, so the pattern emerges from precisely aligned threads. Double ikat, where both warp and weft are pre-dyed, is practised in only a few places worldwide, including Pochampally and Patan.

Jamdani discontinuous weft

Motifs are added by hand during weaving using small bobbins, without a mechanical jacquard — literally embroidering with the weft as the cloth is built, thread by thread.

Zari brocade weaving

Metallic gold or silver-wrapped thread is woven as a supplementary weft to create raised, shimmering motifs, guided historically by punch-card "naksha" patterns — a precursor to the Jacquard loom.

Bandhani tie-and-dye

Cloth is pinched and bound into thousands of tiny knots with thread before dyeing; unbinding reveals a field of small resist-dyed dots forming larger motifs.

Kani twill-tapestry

Used for fine pashmina shawls, this technique interlocks colored wool wefts using small wooden spools (tujis) rather than a shuttle, allowing a painterly build-up of pattern that can take over a year per shawl.

People

Artisan traditions

Handloom weaving in India is overwhelmingly a household and community craft, passed down within weaving lineages rather than taught in institutions.

Skills move within families — a naqshaband (pattern-maker) in Varanasi, a Salvi ikat-dyer in Gujarat, a Padmasali weaver in Telangana — with children often learning by sitting beside a parent at the loom from a young age. Many textiles now carry a Geographical Indication (GI) tag, legally tying the name (Kanchipuram, Pochampally, Chanderi) to production in that specific region by its traditional weavers.

The 2019–20 Fourth All India Handloom Census recorded roughly 31.45 lakh (3.14 million) handloom worker households across the country, over 72% of them women — making handloom one of India's largest sources of women-led rural livelihood.

Why it matters

Cultural significance

Rites of passage

Handloom silks mark weddings, festivals, and rites across India — a Kanjivaram or Banarasi saree is frequently passed down as a family heirloom rather than simply purchased.

Swadeshi & Khadi

Handspun, handwoven khadi cloth became a symbol of self-reliance and resistance during the independence movement, championed by Gandhi as an alternative to British mill cloth.

UNESCO recognition

Techniques including Jamdani weaving have been inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising handloom as a living tradition worth safeguarding.

National Handloom Day

India observes National Handloom Day on 7 August each year — the date chosen to mark the anniversary of the 1905 Swadeshi Movement — to spotlight weavers and encourage domestic demand for handwoven cloth.
