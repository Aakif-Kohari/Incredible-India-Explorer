Forest Fire Hazards of Himachal Pradesh

Chir pine belt · Peak season mid-April to mid-June

Forest covers roughly 27.7% of Himachal Pradesh, and much of the lower and mid-hill belt is dominated by resin-rich chir pine. Every summer, dry pine needles, steep terrain, and hot winds turn a spark — often a careless match or a deliberate needle-clearing burn — into a wildfire that can run for days across whole forest circles.

Geography

Forest regions and fire vulnerability

Himachal's forests run from subtropical chir pine belts in the low valleys to moist oak and deodar stands in the mid-hills and alpine conifers higher still. Fire risk is not evenly spread — it concentrates heavily in one elevation band and one species.

The chir pine belt (low and mid hills)

Chir pine (Pinus roxburghii) dominates the 900–1,800 m band across Kangra, Mandi, Hamirpur, Una, Bilaspur, and Sirmaur districts. Its resin-rich needles, locally called pirul or perul, shed onto the forest floor and form a thick, highly flammable litter layer — the single biggest driver of fire frequency in the state.

Higher, moister forest belts

Above the pine belt, Banj oak, deodar, kail, fir, and spruce forests hold more moisture in their canopy and litter, and burn far less easily. These belts still face occasional fire risk in exceptionally dry years, but the chir pine zone accounts for the overwhelming majority of incidents.

Climate

Fire seasons

The Himachal Pradesh Forest Department's official fire season runs from 15 April to 15 June — the window between winter's end and the arrival of the monsoon, when vegetation is driest, pine-needle litter has fully accumulated, and hot, dry winds sweep the lower hills. Some national comparisons instead track a wider November–June season, which captures early dry spells before the peak.

The scale varies sharply year to year. The 2024–25 season was the worst on record, with 2,613 incidents statewide, 2,433 of them inside the summer window alone. The 2023–24 season recorded 686 fires, affecting 11,032 hectares and causing an estimated ₹3.24 crore in losses. By contrast, unusually heavy May rain in 2025–26 held the state to just 66 incidents by mid-May, showing how strongly a single wet spell can suppress the season.

Reading fire statistics carefully

Ground-reported incident counts (a few hundred to a few thousand a year) and satellite-based fire-point counts (which can run into the tens of thousands for a single season) measure different things. A single sustained fire can register as multiple separate satellite detections, so the two figures are not directly comparable.

Causes

Contributing factors

Almost every forest fire in Himachal Pradesh has a human origin, but the underlying vulnerability was shaped over more than a century of forest management.

  • A colonial-era species shift: Railway expansion from the 1850s onward drove large-scale resin tapping and commercial planting of chir pine, which steadily replaced native, moisture-holding Banj oak forest across the lower and mid hills — a structural change that still shapes fire risk today.
  • Deliberate litter-clearing burns: Villagers traditionally burn accumulated pine needles to clear walking paths and encourage fresh grass growth for grazing livestock; these controlled burns frequently escape into full wildfires.
  • Careless ignition: Discarded cigarettes and smouldering matchsticks are commonly cited causes, especially near roads, picnic spots, and tourist trails through pine forest.
  • Climate stress: Poor winter rainfall, reduced snowfall, and longer dry spells before the monsoon are making Himalayan forests progressively more flammable, according to forest researchers monitoring the region.
  • Steep terrain: Once ignited, fires spread rapidly uphill along slopes, making containment far harder than on flat ground.
  • Underfunded prevention: In some years the Forest Department has been unable to complete planned controlled burning and fire-line maintenance before the season due to budget constraints, leaving fuel loads uncleared.
Environment

Ecological impacts

Between 2001 and 2023, Himachal Pradesh lost about 957 hectares of tree cover directly to fire, alongside a further 4.37 thousand hectares lost to all other drivers combined — a meaningful share of canopy loss attributable to fire alone. Repeated burning degrades the forest floor's ability to hold moisture, reducing groundwater recharge and the flow of mountain springs that many hill communities depend on.

Fires also release black carbon, a pollutant that can settle on nearby Himalayan snow and ice, reducing its reflectivity and accelerating melt — part of a wider set of climate pressures the region is under; Himachal's own State Climate Change Centre recorded glacial lakes nearly doubling from 805 in 2016 to 1,619 in 2022. Over time, researchers project that repeated fire could shrink the range of pine, cedar, and rhododendron forest in the western Himalaya, pushing it further north and to higher elevation.

A self-reinforcing cycle

Fire-adapted chir pine regenerates quickly after a burn, while slower-growing, moisture-holding oak and deodar struggle to recover in repeatedly burned areas — allowing pine, and sometimes invasive weeds, to dominate even more of the landscape after each fire season.

Biodiversity

Wildlife effects

  • Timing overlaps breeding season: The April–June fire window coincides with the flowering and breeding period for many Himalayan species, so fires disrupt reproduction directly rather than only destroying habitat.
  • Habitat loss and fragmentation: Ground-dwelling and understory species lose cover and food sources across burned chir pine and adjoining mixed-forest belts.
  • Pressure on threatened and endemic species: Species with already-narrow ranges in the western Himalaya face compounding pressure when fire removes suitable habitat during a critical life stage.
  • Reduced forage and water: Degraded understory vegetation and drying springs mean less fodder and drinking water for wildlife even in areas that were not directly burned.
Response

Fire detection and response

India's Forest Survey of India (FSI) runs a near-real-time Forest Fire Alert System, detecting active fire hotspots using MODIS and SNPP-VIIRS satellite sensors processed by the National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC), ISRO, with satellite passes roughly six times a day. Alerts are disseminated down to the local beat level, with SMS notifications reaching field forest officials directly.

Ahead of ignition, FSI's Forest Fire Danger Rating System issues pre-fire alerts using a Fire Weather Index — combining fuel moisture, weather forecasts, forest type, terrain, and historical fire records to rate 25 sq km forest grids from low to extreme risk. Researchers note an important limitation: satellite systems detect fires only after they have already started, and a single large, continuous fire can register as several separate detections.

On the ground in Himachal

  • State Forest Department's own Fire Incident Reporting Engine (FIRE) logs incidents by circle and division.
  • Wireless-equipped control rooms coordinate field response during the peak season.
  • Dedicated firefighting units are activated in the most fire-prone divisions.
  • "Bartandars" — designated local forest-user community members — are engaged to support field staff.
Mitigation

Prevention measures

Prevention in Himachal centres on breaking up the fuel that chir pine sheds every year. Around 1.5 lakh hectares of pine forest statewide need periodic controlled burning of accumulated needles, with roughly 50,000 hectares requiring treatment each year — though funding constraints mean this target is not always met. Maintained fire lines — cleared buffer strips that break the continuity of dry litter — remain one of the most effective low-cost containment tools.

Longer term, foresters and researchers point to reducing chir pine monoculture in favour of native Banj oak and mixed broadleaf regeneration, which holds more moisture and burns less readily. Newer livelihood-linked approaches are also emerging: converting collected pine needles (pirul) into commercial biomass briquettes, which removes standing fuel load from the forest floor while creating rural income.

Practical measures

  • Maintain fire lines and complete controlled burns before mid-April, before the season peaks.
  • Track FSI satellite fire alerts alongside state FIRE portal ground reports.
  • Support pine-needle collection and briquetting programs to reduce standing fuel.
  • Avoid open flames, cigarettes, and unattended campfires on forest trails, especially April–June.
  • Encourage native oak and mixed-broadleaf regeneration over further pine expansion.
