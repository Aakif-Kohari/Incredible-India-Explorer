Jog Falls: Raja, Roarer, Rocket & Rani

Dropping 253 meters (829 feet) on the Sharavathi River, Jog Falls is India's second-highest plunge waterfall — a dramatic quartet of separate cascades that thunder through the Western Ghats, their volume shaped as much by the monsoon as by the Linganamakki Dam upstream.

📏 253m (829 ft) Four-Cascade Drop 🌊 Sharavathi River 🏞️ Western Ghats, Shimoga District 🚧 Dam-Regulated Flow
Four Named Cascades

253m Height Visualization

Unlike a single sheet, Jog Falls splits into four distinct, individually named cascades falling side by side down the same cliff face.

Raja

The largest and most majestic of the four, plunging almost vertically in a single unbroken sheet — the iconic image of Jog Falls.

Roarer

True to its name, this cascade forces its way through a narrow rocky channel, producing a loud, churning roar.

Rocket

Water shoots outward in jet-like bursts off the rock face, breaking apart into spray before it reaches the base.

Rani (Lady/White Lady)

The most delicate of the four, spreading into a fine, misty veil as it drifts down the cliff face.

Jog Falls is regularly cited as India's second-highest plunge (single uninterrupted drop) waterfall, and ranks among the tallest in the world by waterfall databases.

Geology & Formation

How the Sharavathi Carved Jog Falls

The falls formed where the Sharavathi River meets a sudden step in the Western Ghats escarpment, en route from the plateau down toward the Arabian Sea coastal plain.

Escarpment Drop

The Sharavathi flows across the Deccan plateau before hitting the abrupt edge of the Western Ghats, plunging in a near-vertical drop toward the coastal lowlands below.

Rock & Rainforest

Hard granitic gneiss bedrock resists erosion unevenly, splitting the flow into separate channels — the origin of the falls' four distinct cascades — all framed by evergreen Western Ghats forest.

Sharavathi Wildlife Sanctuary

The falls lie within the Sharavathi Valley Wildlife Sanctuary, home to lion-tailed macaques, flying squirrels, and rich Western Ghats biodiversity.

Linganamakki Dam Upstream

Built in the 1960s for hydroelectric power, this dam now controls most of the river's flow, meaning the falls' modern volume depends heavily on scheduled water releases.

River-Flow Information

The Sharavathi: From Plateau to Powerhouse

Understanding Jog Falls today means understanding the Linganamakki Dam, since it controls how much of the Sharavathi actually reaches the cliff edge.

🌧️

Natural Catchment

The Sharavathi's headwaters rise in the Western Ghats and swell dramatically with the southwest monsoon between June and September.

🏗️

Linganamakki Reservoir

Most of the river's flow is impounded upstream at Linganamakki Dam and diverted for the Mahatma Gandhi Hydro-Electric Station, one of Karnataka's major power sources.

🚰

Scheduled Releases

Outside heavy monsoon spells, what reaches Jog Falls is largely water released or spilled from the dam, so visible flow can vary day to day depending on power-generation needs.

⚡

Downstream Power Stations

Water passing (or diverted around) Jog Falls continues to power multiple hydroelectric stations along the Sharavathi's descent toward the Arabian Sea.

Travel Seasons

Monsoon vs Dry-Season Comparison

Because flow is dam-regulated, the difference between seasons at Jog Falls is more dramatic than at most Indian waterfalls.

Monsoon (July to September) — Peak Volume

Heavy Western Ghats rainfall pushes the Linganamakki reservoir toward capacity, forcing spillway releases that send all four cascades into full, thunderous flow. This is when Jog Falls looks most like its famous photographs — but mist, slippery viewpoints, and closed lower steps are common.

Safety Information

Visiting Jog Falls Safely

The falls are powerful and the terrain is steep — a few precautions go a long way.

🚫

No Swimming in the Pool

The plunge pool and rocks near the base carry strong currents and sudden depth changes; entering the water is prohibited and dangerous.

🪨

Slippery Rocks & Steps

Spray keeps viewpoint steps and rocks wet year-round; wear grippy footwear and stick to railed paths, especially during monsoon.

⛔

Respect Closed Sections

Lower valley steps are frequently closed by the forest department during heavy flow or after incidents — barriers exist for real safety reasons.

🌩️

Monsoon Weather Awareness

Sudden heavy rain, mist, and slippery approach roads are common June–September; check local conditions before driving up.
