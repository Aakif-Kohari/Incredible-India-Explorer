Dashashwamedh Ghat — Kashi's Beating Heart

The Ghat of Ten Sacrificed Horses

Varanasi's busiest and most celebrated riverfront, Dashashwamedh Ghat sits just steps from the Kashi Vishwanath Temple. By day it hums with pilgrims, boatmen, and vendors; every evening it transforms into the stage for the Ganga Aarti — a synchronized fire ceremony of lamps, bells, conch shells, and chanting that has drawn devotees and travellers here for generations.

📜 Historical Background

Dashashwamedh Ghat is among the oldest and most sacred of Varanasi's riverfront steps. In older Indian scriptures the site was known by an earlier name, Rudrasaras, before it took the name it carries today. The ghat as it stands now was rebuilt in 1748 under the patronage of the Maratha Peshwa Baji Rao I, part of the broader wave of 18th-century Maratha investment in Kashi's sacred geography. It has since been repaired and remodelled several times, with the most recent major renovation completed in 2019.

🐎 Name and Legends

The name Dashashwamedh comes from Sanskrit: dasha (ten), ashwa (horse), and medha (sacrifice) — "the ghat of ten sacrificed horses." According to the most widely told legend, Lord Brahma performed the Dashashwamedh Yajna, a grand ritual sacrifice of ten horses, at this very spot to welcome Lord Shiva to Kashi.

A more elaborate version of the story, drawn from local tradition, links the ghat to King Divodasa, a legendary ruler said to have restored Kashi after a sixty-year drought threatened civilization — with Brahma himself said to have overseen the founding of the city under Divodasa's rule.

A City-Defining Myth

Whichever version is told, the legend positions Dashashwamedh Ghat as a foundational site in Kashi's sacred origin story — not just a bathing ghat, but the place where the city's divine relationship with Shiva is said to have first been sealed.

🪔 The Ganga Aarti Ceremony

Every evening, soon after sunset, seven young Brahmin priests — said to symbolically represent the Sapta Rishis (seven sages) who once offered puja to Maa Ganga — take their places on a raised platform facing the river. In perfect synchrony, they raise and circle large multi-tiered brass lamps called deepams, each weighing roughly 15–20 kg, while conch shells sound and Sanskrit hymns and bhajans rise in praise of Lord Shiva (Vishwanath) and Lord Vishnu.

The ceremony lasts about 45 minutes: around 7 PM in summer's later sunsets, closer to 6 PM in winter. There is no entry fee. Hundreds of devotees and visitors gather on the ghat steps and in boats moored just offshore to watch the rows of flame reflected on the Ganga.

Lamps, Bells, and Chants

The ritual elements are deliberate: fire (the deepams) as the central offering, bells and conch shells to mark rhythm and invoke auspiciousness, incense for purification, and call-and-response chanting that lets the entire crowd participate — not just observe.

🕉️ Religious Importance

Dashashwamedh Ghat's proximity to the Kashi Vishwanath Temple — roughly 650 metres away, connected by the narrow, bustling Vishwanath Gali — makes it central to a well-worn pilgrim ritual: a purifying dip in the Ganga at the ghat, followed by darshan of the Vishwanath Jyotirlinga. A holy bath here is believed to cleanse sin, and pilgrims often pour milk into the river as an offering meant to please the goddess Ganga herself. Once a year, on the tenth waxing day of the lunar month of Jyeshtha (roughly May–June), the ghat also hosts a large-scale festival dedicated to the worship of the Ganga.

🛶 The Riverfront Experience

Dashashwamedh never really rests. Mornings bring pilgrims for ritual bathing and prayer; through the day, boatmen ferry visitors along the ghats, and the surrounding lanes fill with vendors selling flowers, lamps, sweets, and Banarasi silk. As dusk falls, the ghat reorients entirely around the Aarti — seating fills, boats gather just off the steps for a water-level view, and the narrow Vishwanath Gali behind the ghat is at its most crowded, alive with street food stalls and shoppers on their way to or from the temple.

💡 Cultural & Historical Facts

  • The ghat's earlier scriptural name was Rudrasaras, before it became Dashashwamedh.
  • Rebuilt in its present form in 1748 by the Maratha Peshwa, Baji Rao I.
  • Kashi Vishwanath Temple is roughly 650 m (about 4 minutes' walk) from the ghat.
  • Manikarnika Ghat lies about 950 m away, along the same riverfront.
  • The Ganga Aarti is performed by seven priests, symbolizing the Sapta Rishis.
  • Special, larger Aartis are held on Tuesdays and during major religious festivals.
  • The most recent major renovation of the ghat was completed in 2019.
