History

From Murumbadevi Dongar to the Maratha capital

Long before it bore the name Rajgad, the hill was known as Murumbadevi Dongar, a modest hilltop stronghold that had already changed hands between local dynasties. Its first recorded capture dates back to the late fifteenth century, but the hill only became historically decisive in 1647, when a young Shivaji Maharaj took it and began transforming it into a fortress capable of anchoring a kingdom.

Treasures and building material recovered from the neighbouring fort of Torna were used to raise Rajgad's walls, bastions and gateways. Over the following decades the fort weathered repeated Mughal and Adilshahi offensives without ever falling, a record that helped cement its reputation as unconquerable.

After Shivaji's death in 1680, Rajgad continued to change hands through the turbulent years of Maratha, Mughal and eventually British contention, before it was finally taken by the British in the early nineteenth century and left to the elements.

Builder

Shaped by Shivaji Maharaj

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj personally oversaw Rajgad's expansion after capturing it in 1647, turning a modest hill fort into a fortified capital with three radiating machis, a walled citadel, granaries, water cisterns and administrative quarters. The fort's layout reflects his approach to military engineering: every approach path is covered by a bastion, and every bastion covers another.

Capital of the Maratha Empire

Twenty-six years at the centre of power

From 1647 until 1674, Rajgad served as the nerve centre of Maratha administration and military planning. It was here that campaigns were plotted, that wealth captured during the 1664 raid on Surat was safeguarded, and that pivotal family events unfolded — including the birth of Shivaji's son Rajaram and the death of his wife Saibai. When Shivaji was crowned Chhatrapati in 1674, the capital moved to Raigad Fort, but Rajgad's role as the cradle of the empire was never eclipsed.

Architecture

One citadel, three machis

Rajgad's defining feature is its trident-shaped plan: a central citadel, the Balekilla, sits at the summit while three long fortified ridges — the machis — radiate outward, each independently walled and each able to hold out even if the others fell.

Padmavati Machi

The largest of the three, home to the Padmavati Temple, granaries, and the ruins of Shivaji's residential quarters and Rajaram's birthplace.

Suvela Machi

A narrow, dramatically fortified spur with double-walled defences and hidden posterns, considered the most heavily engineered of the three ridges.

Sanjeevani Machi

The longest machi, ringed with an extensive bastion network built to guard the routes toward Torna Fort.

Balekilla

The citadel proper, reached through the imposing Maha Darwaja, containing the fort's highest point and commanding views over all three machis.

Trekking Route

Getting to the summit

Most trekkers begin from Gunjavane village, the shortest and most popular route, climbing past the Chor Darwaja (thief's gate) directly onto Padmavati Machi in roughly two to three hours. A longer, more demanding route starts from Pali village and approaches via the Suvela Machi side, rewarding trekkers with the fort's most dramatic fortifications along the way.

The best time to visit is between October and February, when the weather is cool and visibility is highest; the monsoon months bring lush greenery and waterfalls but slippery basalt steps.
