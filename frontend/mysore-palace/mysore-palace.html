Mysore Palace Explorer

The official residence of the Wadiyar dynasty and the seat of the Kingdom of Mysore, Amba Vilas Palace — popularly known as Mysore Palace — is one of India's most magnificent royal residences. Its Indo-Saracenic silhouette, gilded domes, and dazzling Dasara illuminations make it the cultural heart of Karnataka's royal heritage.

Palace Profile

Location, Legacy & Significance

The Crown Jewel of Mysuru

Mysore Palace stands at the heart of Mysuru city in Karnataka, facing the Chamundi Hills to the east. Located at the junction of Sayyaji Rao Road and B.N. Road, it is the official residence of the Wadiyar family — the erstwhile maharajas of Mysore — and one of the most visited monuments in India, second only to the Taj Mahal in annual footfall.

The current structure, known as Amba Vilas Palace, was commissioned after the old wooden palace was gutted by fire in 1897 during the wedding of Princess Jayalakshmi Ammani. The 24-year-old Maharaja Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV entrusted its rebuilding to British architect Henry Irwin, and the new palace was completed in 1912, later expanded in 1940 under Maharaja Jayachamarajendra Wadiyar.

Today the palace remains a living royal residence — the Wadiyar family still occupies a private wing — while its public halls welcome millions of visitors each year as a museum of Karnataka's royal past.

Royal Lineage

History of the Wadiyar Dynasty

1399

Yaduraya Wodeyar establishes the Wadiyar dynasty at Mysore, founding a royal line that would rule the region for over five centuries — one of India's longest-reigning royal families.

1610

Raja Wadiyar I captures Srirangapatna and makes it his capital, adopting the Vijayanagara regalia and expanding the kingdom's power across southern Karnataka.

1799

After the fall of Tipu Sultan in the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, the British restore the Wadiyars to the throne. Krishnaraja Wadiyar III shifts the capital back from Srirangapatna to Mysore.

1897

A devastating fire destroys the old palace during a royal wedding. Queen Regent Kempananjammanni Vanivilasa Sannidhana commissions its reconstruction on a grander scale.

1912

The new Amba Vilas Palace is completed under Maharaja Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV — often remembered as the "Saint King" whose reign ushered in Mysore's golden era.

1950

The princely state accedes to the Indian Union. The Wadiyars continue as Rajapramukhs and later custodians of the palace, preserving its rituals and traditions to this day.

Design & Craftsmanship

Indo-Saracenic Architecture

🏛️

A Fusion of Styles

The palace is a masterwork of Indo-Saracenic design — a harmonious blend of Hindu, Islamic, Rajput, and Gothic elements. Henry Irwin combined domes, arches, colonnades, and chhajjas with deep-eaved roofs that echo Assamese and Rajasthani traditions.

👑

The Gilded Central Dome

Crowning the palace is a magnificent central dome of polished white marble, sheathed in gold and rising about 145 feet above the courtyard. Flanked by two smaller domes, it anchors the three-storey grey granite facade that stretches 245 feet along its frontage.

🗼

Exterior Grandeur

Five-storey towers capped with pinnacles rise at the cardinal points of the facade. Ornate scalloped arches, slender minarets, and deep pink marble domes give the exterior its unmistakable silhouette against the Chamundi Hills skyline.

🎨

Decorative Artwork

Interiors shimmer with stained-glass ceilings, mirrored mosaic floors, bejewelled ivory doors, and murals by Raja Ravi Varma depicting Dussehra processions. Intricate stone-carved screens and gilded pillars showcase the finest craftsmanship of early 20th-century India.

Inside the Palace

Royal Interiors

Amba Vilas Durbar Hall

The private durbar hall where the maharaja held court, adorned with a ceiling of peacock motifs in stained glass, sculpted pillars of granite and gilded metal, and a majestic golden throne (Simhasana) displayed during Dasara.

Public Durbar Hall (Sajje Koti)

An open-air pavilion on the upper floor where the maharaja addressed the public during ceremonial occasions, framed by cast-iron pillars sourced from Glasgow.

Gombe Thotti (Doll Pavilion)

An entrance gallery showcasing traditional dolls, European marble sculptures, and a ceremonial golden howdah once used to carry the maharaja atop an elephant during Dasara processions.

Kalyana Mantapa

The marriage hall dazzles with a giant octagonal stained-glass ceiling of peacock designs, glazed tiled flooring, and a jewelled marriage throne — a favourite stop for every visitor.

Royal Portrait Gallery

Halls lined with paintings of the Wadiyar lineage and monumental canvases by Raja Ravi Varma, alongside historic armaments, royal costumes, and the palace's celebrated collection of musical instruments.

Festival of Lights

Mysore Dasara Celebrations

For ten days each autumn, Mysore Palace becomes the stage for Nada Habba — the state festival of Karnataka. The tradition began in 1610 when Raja Wadiyar I instituted grand Vijayadashami celebrations at Srirangapatna, and the Wadiyars have presided over the festival ever since.

  • Illuminations: Nearly 100,000 bulbs trace the palace's outline every evening of the festival, creating its most iconic spectacle.
  • Jamboo Savari: On Vijayadashami, the idol of Goddess Chamundeshwari is carried through the city in the golden howdah atop a decorated elephant, led traditionally by the scion of the royal family.
  • Cultural Programs: The palace forecourts host classical music and dance performances, wrestling bouts, and a famous torchlight parade by the police.
  • Sunday Evenings: Even outside Dasara, the palace glows with illuminations every Sunday evening and on national holidays.

Living Heritage

Cultural Significance

Mysore Palace is far more than a monument — it is the beating heart of Karnataka's identity. It anchors the city's nickname "City of Palaces," lends its silhouette to the state's tourism emblem, and continues to shape the rhythm of Mysuru's civic and religious life.

As the seat of the Wadiyars, who were renowned patrons of art, music, and learning, the palace nurtured the Mysore school of painting, Carnatic music traditions, and the craft clusters that made the region famous — from sandalwood carving to Channapatna toys. Its preservation today supports these living crafts and draws scholars, photographers, and travellers from around the world.

In recognition of its heritage value, the palace has been adopted under the Government of India's "Adopt a Heritage" scheme, ensuring its care for generations to come.

Why It Matters

  • Symbol of over 600 years of continuous royal patronage
  • Epicentre of Nada Habba — Karnataka's state festival
  • Patron of Mysore painting, Carnatic music, and traditional crafts
  • Among the most visited monuments in India after the Taj Mahal
  • A working museum safeguarding royal artefacts and archives

Visitor Information

Location

Agrahara, Chamrajpura, Mysuru, Karnataka 570004 — about 2 km from Mysuru Junction railway station.

Best Season

October to March; visit during Dasara (Sep–Oct) for the full illumination experience.

Visiting Hours

10:00 AM – 5:30 PM for interiors (last ticket ~5:15 PM); grounds remain accessible for evening light shows.

Light & Sound Show

Evening narration of the palace's history in Kannada and English on select days — check schedules locally.

Getting There

Mysuru is well connected by rail and road from Bengaluru (~145 km); the nearest airport is Mysuru Airport with links to Bengaluru.
