Rukhmabai Raut

The Doctor Who Challenged Child Marriage

Born: 1864, Bombay
Died: 1955, Bombay
Profession: Physician
Legacy: First practising Indian woman doctor

Early Life

Rukhmabai was born in 1864 in Bombay (now Mumbai) into a family that belonged to the carpenter (Suthar) community. Her father, Janardan Pandurang, was a man who valued education and progressive ideas. After her mother, Jayantibai, passed away when Rukhmabai was young, her father married again. Her stepmother, Jaibai, became a strong and supportive figure in her life, encouraging her education at a time when girls' schooling was virtually unheard of.

Rukhmabai's early education took place at home, where she learned to read and write Marathi and English. She was deeply influenced by the reformist ideas of the time, particularly those of the Brahmo Samaj and the newly formed Arya Mahila Samaj (Arya Women's Society). This intellectual foundation would later empower her to challenge the deeply entrenched social norms of her era.

Child Marriage in 19th-Century India

In 19th-century colonial India, child marriage was a widespread and socially sanctioned practice, particularly among upper-caste Hindu communities. Girls were often married off before or around the onset of puberty — sometimes as young as eight or nine years old. The custom was rooted in patriarchal norms, concerns about "purity," and the strategic formation of family alliances.

Reformist movements, led by figures like Raja Ram Mohan Roy and later Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, had successfully campaigned for the abolition of sati (1829) and the legalisation of widow remarriage (1856). However, child marriage proved far more resistant to reform. The first Age of Consent Act (1860) set the age of consent at 10 years, but it was poorly enforced and did not apply to marriages that had already taken place. Into this volatile social landscape stepped Rukhmabai.

The Legal Case

At the age of 11, Rukhmabai was married to Dadaji Bhikaji, a man nearly 20 years her senior. She continued to live with her father, who provided for her education and upbringing. Dadaji, however, lived a life of relative idleness and dependency. Years later, when Dadaji demanded that Rukhmabai come to live with him as his wife, she refused. She argued that she had been married as a child without her consent and that the union was invalid.

Dadaji took the matter to the Bombay High Court, filing a suit for "restitution of conjugal rights" — a legal provision under which a spouse could compel the other to return to the matrimonial home. The case, Dadaji Bhikaji v. Rukhmabai (1885), became a landmark in Indian legal history.

Justice Charles Pinhey initially ruled in Rukhmabai's favour, holding that she could not be compelled to live with a man she had not chosen to marry, especially since the marriage had been performed when she was a child and had not been consummated. However, upon appeal, the full bench of the Bombay High Court reversed the decision in 1886. The court held that under Hindu law, a marriage — even one performed when the girl was a child — was a valid and binding contract. Rukhmabai was ordered to live with Dadaji or face imprisonment.

Rather than submit, Rukhmabai chose to go to prison. This extraordinary defiance of both the court and patriarchal custom sparked a massive public debate and eventually led to the passage of the Age of Consent Act of 1891, which raised the age of consent from 10 to 12.

The Public Debate

The Rukhmabai case ignited a firestorm in the Indian and British press. Reformists hailed her as a heroine of women's rights. Behramji Malabari, a prominent Parsi journalist and social reformer, used her case to campaign for legislation against child marriage. Pandita Ramabai, another pioneering woman reformer, publicly supported Rukhmabai's stand.

On the other side, conservative sections of society and orthodox religious leaders defended the practice of child marriage as a sacred Hindu tradition. They argued that British courts had no right to interfere in Hindu personal law. The debate exposed the tension between colonial modernity and indigenous tradition, and forced the British government to confront the contradiction between its stated civilising mission and its reluctance to interfere in native religious customs.

Journey to Medical Education

Rukhmabai's legal battle caught the attention of the Countess of Dufferin, who was leading a campaign to provide medical care to women in India (the Dufferin Fund). With the support of this fund, Rukhmabai left for England in 1889 to pursue a medical education — a remarkable feat for a woman of her background and the era.

She enrolled at the London School of Medicine for Women, one of the few institutions in the world at the time that trained women as physicians. She graduated in 1894, becoming one of the first Indian women to qualify as a doctor in the Western medical tradition.

Medical Career

Upon returning to India in 1894, Rukhmabai was appointed as a physician at the Cama Hospital for Women and Children in Bombay. She was the first Indian woman to formally practise medicine in the country. During her long career, she treated thousands of women who, due to pardah (seclusion) norms, would not have been able to see a male doctor.

Rukhmabai also served in the princely state of Surat and worked extensively in women's health, focusing on maternal care, midwifery training, and the treatment of infectious diseases. She was a tireless advocate for women's education, writing letters to newspapers under the pen name "A Hindu Lady" even before her legal battle.

Social Reform & Legacy

Rukhmabai Raut's legacy extends far beyond her medical career. Her refusal to submit to a child marriage and her willingness to face imprisonment set a powerful precedent. Her case directly contributed to the passage of the Age of Consent Act of 1891, the first legislative attempt to curb child marriage in India.

She continued to write and speak on women's issues throughout her life. She died in 1955 at the age of 91, having witnessed India's independence and the passage of the Hindu Code Bills, which finally codified women's rights to divorce and inheritance — the very rights she had been denied.
