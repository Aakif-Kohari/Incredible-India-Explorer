Royal Riverfront Heritage Varanasi, Uttar Pradesh

Rewa Ghat

A Royal Riverfront Legacy on the Ganges

Tucked quietly between Assi Ghat and Tulsi Ghat, Rewa Ghat carries the layered history of a priest's endowment, a Punjab king's court, and a Baghela Maharaja's patronage. Once known as Lala Mishir Ghat, it was bought by the royal house of Rewa in 1879 and rebuilt in sandstone as the Rewa Kothi — a palace that today, quietly and fittingly, houses the music and art students of Banaras Hindu University.

Location West bank of the Ganges, between Assi Ghat and Tulsi Ghat, Varanasi, Uttar Pradesh
River Ganges (Ganga) — near its confluence with the Assi River
Nearest Landmark ~5 km from Varanasi Junction (Cantt.) Railway Station; close to Banaras Hindu University

📜 Origin & History

Lala Mishir Ghat, the Earliest Name

Rewa Ghat did not begin as a royal riverfront at all. It was first built by Lala Mishir (Lala Misir), the family priest (purohit) of Maharaja Ranjit Singh of Punjab — founder of the Sikh Empire and known as the "Lion of Punjab." In its earliest years the ghat carried his name: Lala Mishir Ghat.

A Second Name: Leelaram Ghat

Before its royal transformation, the ghat passed through another identity as Leelaram (Lilaram) Ghat. Its position was originally understood as part of the wider stretch of Assi Ghat, the southern anchor of Varanasi's riverfront, rather than a distinct site in its own right.

From Priest's Endowment to Princely Estate

The shift from a priest's modest riverfront foundation to a full princely estate came in 1879, when the ghat and its mansion were purchased outright by the royal house of Rewa — the moment that gives the ghat its present name and its enduring architectural character.

👑 Rewa Royal Heritage

The Maharaja of Rewa (1879)

In 1879, the ghat was purchased by the Maharaja of Rewa, ruler of the Rewa princely state in present-day Madhya Pradesh. The reigning Baghela-dynasty monarch at that date was Maharaja Raghuraj Singh (r. 1854–1880), a patron known across Kashi for endowing riverfront property in the sacred city, as many princely houses did to secure merit and a permanent presence at Varanasi.

The Baghela Dynasty of Rewa

Rewa State was the seat of the Baghela dynasty, rulers of the Baghelkhand region for centuries from their capital at Rewa (Madhya Pradesh). Like many princely houses of the era, the Rewa court invested in temples, ghats, and dharamshalas in Varanasi as an expression of piety and prestige, well beyond their own territory.

Renamed in the Royal House's Honour

Following the purchase, the site was renamed after its new patrons — recorded variously in local sources as Rewa Ghat or Rewan Ghat — and repairs and reconstruction were carried out under the Rewa court's direction, replacing the earlier priest's endowment with a formal princely riverfront residence.

🏛️ Explore Rewa Ghat

Rewa Kothi: A Sandstone Riverfront Palace

The palace built by the Rewa court, known locally as the Rewa Kothi, stands at the top of the stairs on the northern edge of Ganga Mahal — immediately recognisable by the royal insignia carved above the gate facing the river.

  • Sandstone Construction: Built almost entirely in dressed sandstone, in keeping with the riverfront palace tradition of Varanasi's princely ghats.
  • Two Linked Structures: The complex comprises a main palace block and an auxiliary house, connected by covered northern and southern porches resting on joined stone pillars.
  • Ardhstambhs in the Coastline Wall: A collection of ardhstambhs (half-pillars), salvaged and reused, forms part of the palace's riverside retaining wall — a distinctive structural signature of the site.

🏛️ Architectural Note

Unlike the colonnaded grandeur of some neighbouring princely ghats, Rewa Kothi's architecture is comparatively restrained — solid sandstone masonry built for residence and administration rather than public ceremony.

From Royal Residence to Arts Hostel

In the second half of the 20th century, the Maharaja of Rewa ("Rewa Naresh") donated the Rewa Kothi to Banaras Hindu University (then referred to as Kashi Hindu Vishwavidyalaya). The university converted the palace into a hostel for students of its Drishya Kala (Visual Arts) and Sangeet Kala (Music) faculties — a transformation that continues to shape the ghat's identity today.

  • A Living Arts Community: Rewa Ghat is still known locally as a favourite haunt of musicians and painters, many of whom arrive in the early morning to sketch and paint the riverfront's changing light.
  • Quiet Among Kashi's Riverfront: Set away from the crowds of Dashashwamedh and Assi, it is often described as one of Varanasi's cleanest and calmest ghats — valued for reflection as much as for its history.
  • A Rare Continuity: Few princely riverfront estates in Varanasi remain in continuous, active use; Rewa Ghat's life as a functioning student hostel is an unusually direct link between its royal past and its present.

🎨 Cultural Highlight

A palace endowed by a Punjab court priest, purchased by a Madhya Pradesh royal house, and now home to Varanasi's own art and music students — Rewa Ghat's story crosses three regions of India in one riverfront address.

Its Place in the Riverfront Chain

Rewa Ghat sits inside one of the most historically dense stretches of Varanasi's riverfront, immediately south of the city's most famous southern anchor point.

  • Assi Ghat (south end of the chain): Rewa Ghat was originally considered part of Assi Ghat, where the Assi River meets the Ganges — one of the city's most auspicious bathing points.
  • Ganga Mahal Ghat (immediate neighbour): Named for a former Maharaja's palace of its own, Ganga Mahal Ghat sits directly beside Rewa Ghat, with the Rewa Kothi standing at its northern edge.
  • Tulsi Ghat (just north): A short walk away, Tulsi Ghat marks the site associated with Goswami Tulsidas, who is said to have composed the Ramcharitmanas nearby and founded a Hanuman shrine just above the ghat.

🗺️ Orientation Tip

Visitors walking the riverfront from Assi Ghat northward toward Dashashwamedh Ghat pass Rewa Ghat within the first few minutes — easy to miss, but worth the pause.

🛕 Temples & Sacred Surroundings

A Riverfront of Residence, Not Ritual

Rewa Ghat itself is not built around a single grand temple, unlike some of Varanasi's more famous ghats — its story is one of a priest's endowment and a royal palace rather than a public shrine. Its sacred character instead comes from the temples that frame it on either side along the riverfront.

Tulsi Ghat's Hanuman Shrine

Just north of Rewa Ghat, Tulsi Ghat carries one of the most literarily significant sacred sites on the river: the monastery and Hanuman temple founded by Goswami Tulsidas, the saint-poet who composed the Awadhi Ramcharitmanas in this stretch of Kashi.

Kedar Ghat's Kedareshwar Temple

A short distance further along the riverfront, Kedar Ghat is home to the Kedareshwar Temple, one of the significant Shiva shrines of Varanasi and a popular pilgrimage stop for visitors walking this southern stretch of ghats.

💡 Interesting Facts

  • Three names, one ghat: Before becoming Rewa Ghat in 1879, it was known first as Lala Mishir Ghat and later as Leelaram (Lilaram) Ghat.
  • Built by a court priest, not a king: The original ghat was endowed by Lala Mishir, priest to Maharaja Ranjit Singh of Punjab — the founder of the Sikh Empire — long before any connection to Rewa existed.
  • A palace made partly of salvaged pillars: The Rewa Kothi's riverside retaining wall incorporates a collection of ardhstambhs (half-pillars), a distinctive construction detail rarely seen elsewhere on the riverfront.
  • Donated, not sold, to its present use: The Maharaja of Rewa gifted the palace to Banaras Hindu University rather than selling it — an act that kept the site in continuous public use.
  • A working arts hostel today: Rewa Kothi houses BHU's Visual Arts (Drishya Kala) and Music (Sangeet Kala) students, making it one of the few princely ghat-palaces in Varanasi still in active daily use.
  • About 5 km from Varanasi Junction: Close enough to the city's main railway station for an easy visit, yet the ghat remains one of the quietest stretches of the riverfront.
