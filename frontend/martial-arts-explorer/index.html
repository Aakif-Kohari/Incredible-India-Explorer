Traditional Martial Arts of India

Long before organised sport, India trained warriors in earthen pits, temple courtyards and village grounds. Some of those systems are still taught today. Others are held by a handful of ageing masters. This is a guide to twelve of them — what they train, what they fight with, and how close each one is to disappearing.

Where It Comes From

A Shared Root, Many Branches

India's martial systems developed separately in different regions, but they share a common vocabulary of ideas.

The classical Indian body of knowledge on weapons and warfare is called Dhanurveda. It is not the source of every Indian martial art, but its categories — how weapons are classified, how a fighter is conditioned, how combat relates to breath and discipline — recur across systems that otherwise have nothing to do with each other.

What varies is the terrain and the war that shaped each one. Mardani Khel favours short, fast weapon work because it was practised in the narrow corridors of Sahyadri hill forts. Silambam uses continuous circular motion because a staff had to hold off several attackers on open ground. Kalaripayattu puts bare-handed combat last in the syllabus, because striking a marma vital point safely requires knowledge you only get after years of weapon work.

Almost all of them were disrupted in the same way. Colonial-era restrictions on arms and on martial communities pushed weapon practice out of public life. What survived often survived as performance — festival display, temple ritual, wedding entertainment — with the combat content quietly folded away inside it.

  • Training is usually staged: conditioning first, weapons second, bare hands last
  • Most systems pair a fighting art with a healing or massage tradition
  • Rhythm — drum, chant or breath — is a teaching tool, not decoration
  • Transmission is teacher-to-student and in person, which is why lineages are fragile
🕉️

Why "Oldest" Is Complicated

You will often read that Kalaripayattu is the world's oldest martial art. What can actually be said is narrower: Tamil and Malayalam literature contains some of the earliest surviving descriptions of organised armed training anywhere, and the practices described are recognisably continuous with what is taught now.
