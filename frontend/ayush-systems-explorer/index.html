🌿 Six systems, one ministry

AYUSH Traditional Medicine Systems

India is unusual in giving six traditional medical systems statutory recognition, their own ministry, their own regulators and their own pharmacopoeias. They are not variants of one another — they come from different places, reason from different theories, and diagnose in different ways. This page treats them separately, and says where the evidence for each stands.

The Arrangement

Six Systems Under One Roof

The grouping is administrative. Treating it as evidence that the systems share a common theory is the most common mistake made about AYUSH.

The Ministry of AYUSH was created in November 2014, upgrading a department that had existed since 1995. What it administers is a genuinely mixed set. Ayurveda and Siddha are indigenous but textually distinct — one in Sanskrit, one in Tamil, attributed to different lineages. Unani is Greco-Arabic and arrived with the Delhi Sultanate. Homoeopathy is late-eighteenth-century German. Yoga and naturopathy were paired administratively despite yoga being an Indian philosophical discipline and naturopathy a nineteenth-century European movement. Sowa-Rigpa is Tibetan, and was recognised in 2010 — after the acronym had already been fixed, which is why it has no letter in it.

What they do share is institutional treatment: statutory regulators, five-and-a-half-year undergraduate degrees, government hospitals and dispensaries, published pharmacopoeias, and drug licensing under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act with their own manufacturing standards.

That shared apparatus is worth noticing, because it means these are not folk practices operating outside the state. They are regulated professions with examinations, registers and manufacturing inspections — which also means failures of regulation in one of them are failures of a public system, not of tradition.

  • Six systems, four separate research councils, two statutory regulators
  • Undergraduate degrees run five and a half years including internship
  • Drugs licensed under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, with GMP under Schedule T
  • Official pharmacopoeias published by a dedicated commission

On Comparison

Comparing these systems to each other, or to biomedicine, using a single yardstick does not work well. They define illness differently, so they are not answering the same question in different ways — in several cases they are answering different questions. Where this page compares them, it compares specific things: origin, theory, diagnostic protocol, materia medica and degree awarded.
