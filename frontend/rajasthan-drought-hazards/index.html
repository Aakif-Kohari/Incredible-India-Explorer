Drought Hazards of Rajasthan

Thar Desert to the Aravallis · Recurring meteorological drought

Rajasthan covers about a tenth of India’s land but holds little more than one percent of its water. Most of the state is arid or semi-arid, so a weak southwest monsoon quickly becomes crop failure, empty tanks, and stressed groundwater.

Landscape

Arid and semi-arid geography

The Aravalli range runs roughly northeast–southwest and divides a hyper-arid west from a slightly wetter east. West of the hills lies the Thar (Great Indian) Desert: sand dunes, rocky pavements, high summer heat, dry winds, and evaporation that far exceeds rainfall.

Western Rajasthan

Jaisalmer, Barmer, Bikaner, Jodhpur, and neighbouring districts are arid to semi-arid. Potential evapotranspiration averages about 2,000 mm a year, so even a “normal” monsoon cannot refill soils and aquifers for long. Humidity is low and rain, when it comes, often falls in a few heavy bursts.

Eastern Rajasthan

East of the Aravallis the climate is semi-arid to sub-humid. Rainfall is higher, winds are weaker, and cropping is more diverse — but drought still occurs when the monsoon is late, patchy, or ends early. About 60–75% of the state is classified arid or semi-arid.

Climate

Rainfall patterns

Almost all rain arrives with the southwest monsoon (June–September). State mean annual rainfall is about 490 mm, with published averages also cited near 575 mm depending on the period and stations used. The gradient is steep: about 100 mm in northwestern Jaisalmer, over 400 mm along the western Aravalli fringe (Sikar, Jhunjhunu, Pali), about 550 mm at Ajmer, over 1,000 mm in Jhalawar, and about 1,638 mm at Mount Abu — the wettest point in the state.

Year-to-year variability is highest in the west. Dry spells inside the monsoon, and the occasional cloudburst from a passing low-pressure system, both belong to the same climate. IMD treats a large seasonal rainfall deficit as meteorological drought; hydrology and crops can fail even when the annual total looks only moderately low.

Why drought sets in

Rain is low, late, or poorly timed for sowing. Heat and wind raise evaporative demand. Soils are sandy and hold little moisture. Groundwater is deep, often brackish, and slow to recharge. Those conditions stacked together turn a weak monsoon into agricultural and hydrological drought.

Hotspots

Major drought-prone regions

More than 60% of Rajasthan’s districts fall in high drought-risk zones. Hazard mapping with the Standardized Precipitation Index points especially to the north and northwest.

  • Hyper-arid west: Jaisalmer and Barmer — lowest rainfall, most frequent severe droughts.
  • Arid west-central: Jodhpur, Bikaner, Nagaur, Jalore, Pali — rainfed kharif and livestock economies.
  • North / canal fringe: Ganganagar, Hanumangarh, and western Bikaner — irrigated in places, still drought-prone when canals and monsoon both fail.
  • Shekhawati and east-central: Sikar, Jhunjhunu, Jaipur — SPI-based maps still rank them among the more drought-prone districts.
Water

Water scarcity

Surface water

Rivers are few and seasonal. Village ponds (nadi, talab) and check dams fill only after rain and often dry before summer. The Indira Gandhi Canal has transformed parts of the northwest, but most desert settlements still depend on stored rain and tankers in bad years.

Groundwater

Wells are the main source of drinking water and irrigation where canals do not reach. Recharge is slow; over-extraction has dropped water tables in many western districts. In large parts of the desert, remaining groundwater is brackish, so families store rain in covered cisterns rather than rely on the aquifer.

Livelihoods

Agricultural and ecosystem impact

About 70% of Rajasthan’s people depend on farming and related work. A failed kharif season hits both grain and fodder, then livestock, then wage labour as migrants leave the village.

Crops and herds

Pearl millet (bajra), moth bean, cluster bean (guar), sorghum, and pulses are the rainfed staples of the west. Sowing windows are short. Drought cuts yields, forces farmers onto poorer seed, and empties grazing land. Small and marginal holders with little irrigation are the most exposed. Canal and tube-well areas can still lose a crop if power, canal roster, or late rain fail together.

Ecosystems

Grasslands and desert scrub lose cover; dune movement increases; wildlife and livestock concentrate on remaining water. Seasonal wetlands and village tanks that support birds and recharge wells stay empty. Repeated drought plus over-grazing and groundwater decline make recovery slower each cycle.

Record

Past drought examples

  • 1901–2003: Western Rajasthan recorded about 20 moderate droughts (50–75% of normal rain) and 10 severe droughts (below 50% of normal), versus 14 moderate and 5 severe in the east.
  • 1918: Among the worst monsoon failures of the twentieth century in western Rajasthan; Jodhpur’s kharif rain was among the lowest on record (about 37 mm at one long-term station).
  • 1987: Severe drought across western tehsils; 54 of 72 tehsils received scanty rainfall. A widely cited recent benchmark, with the 2002 event.
  • 2002: Jodhpur recorded about 91 mm for the year (roughly 23% of its long-term average). In western Rajasthan, 72 of 90 tehsils had scanty rain and none were in the “normal” class. Khadin farmers who had stored runoff still grew sorghum fodder while many rainfed fields failed.
Heritage

Traditional water-management practices

Catch and store

Johads are small earthen check dams that slow runoff and recharge wells. Nadi / talab are community ponds with an agore (embankment) and overflow. Tankas are covered underground cisterns for drinking water, essential where groundwater is saline. Beris are shallow percolation wells in sandy tracts.

Farm the flood

Khadins, used in Jaisalmer since the Paliwal period (about the 16th century), hold rocky-catchment runoff behind an embankment so crops grow on receding soil moisture. Baoris (stepwells) tap shallow groundwater and stored seepage. These systems still work, but they cannot meet today’s demand without repair, desilting, and limits on pumping.

Response

Drought preparedness

IMD monitors rainfall and issues drought-related assessments. Rajasthan’s Weather Watch Group (Relief, Agriculture, IMD, irrigation, PHED, groundwater) meets through the monsoon to track onset. Declaration of drought unlocks relief: drinking-water tankers, fodder depots, employment works, and input support. National guidance sits in the NDMA drought management framework and in agricultural research from CAZRI, Jodhpur.

Longer-term preparedness is local: keep johads and tankas in repair before June, shift to less water-intensive crops, protect common grazing, recharge groundwater instead of only extracting it, and treat canal water as finite. Early warning only helps if seed, fodder, and drinking water are already staged.

Practical measures

  • Desilt nadis and johads before the monsoon; repair tanka covers.
  • Track IMD district rainfall, not only the state average.
  • Store fodder and drinking water as soon as monsoon onset looks late.
  • Prefer millet, pulses, and khadin farming on rainfed land.
  • Limit new tube-wells where aquifers are falling or saline.
