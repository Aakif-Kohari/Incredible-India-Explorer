Sacred Groves of India

Long before there was forest law, there were patches of forest nobody would cut. A deity lived there, and that was enough. India has an estimated hundred thousand or more of them — and in many landscapes they are the last intact forest left.

The Idea

Conservation Without a Conservation Law

A sacred grove is a patch of forest set aside on religious grounds and protected by community rule rather than by statute.

The mechanism is simple and unusually durable. A grove is understood to belong to a deity, not to a person. Because no individual owns it, no individual can sell it, clear it or divide it among heirs. The rules governing it — what may be taken, who may enter, when — are held orally and enforced socially. The sanction for breaking them is not a fine but misfortune.

That structure has protected forest through centuries in which almost everything around it was converted. In Kodagu, in the Khasi hills, on the Chotanagpur plateau, the grove is frequently the only intact vegetation left in its valley, and therefore the only reference for what the landscape once was.

It is worth being precise about what makes this work, because it is easy to romanticise. Groves are not protected because communities held an abstract conservation ethic. They are protected because a specific prohibition, attached to a specific place and a specific deity, was enforced by people who lived beside it. When the belief weakens, the protection goes with it — usually within a generation.

  • Ownership rests with the deity, so the land cannot be sold or partitioned
  • Rules are oral, local and highly specific — they differ from grove to grove
  • Enforcement is social; formal law arrived only in the last few decades
  • Sizes range from a household plot to several thousand hectares
📊

On the Numbers

There is no complete national inventory of sacred groves. Published estimates commonly range from about 100,000 to 150,000, and some go considerably higher. Coverage is very uneven between states — Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Meghalaya are relatively well surveyed, while large parts of central and northern India are not. Treat all counts on this page as indicative.
