Introduction

The Black Leaf Monkey of the Ghats

The Nilgiri Langur (Semnopithecus johnii), also known as the Hooded Leaf Monkey or Black Langur, is a lutung-like primate inhabiting high-altitude rainforests. It is recognized by its lustrous black body fur contrasted with a golden-yellowish head tuft and long agile tail.

As specialized folivores, Nilgiri Langurs possess complex multi-chambered stomachs lined with cellulose-fermenting bacteria that allow them to digest tough leaves and plant toxins unavailable to other primates.

Key Highlights

  • Distinct Appearance: Glossy dark fur with a reddish-brown or golden crown.
  • Vocal Communicator: Produces deep, booming "hoo-hoo" territorial calls heard across valleys.
  • Shola Indicator: Essential seed disperser and leaf consumer in Shola-grassland mosaics.
  • Legal Protection: Listed under Schedule I of India's Wildlife Protection Act.

Scientific Name & Taxonomy

Formally classified as Semnopithecus johnii (formerly Trachypithecus johnii), named in honor of 19th-century naturalist Rev. C. S. John.

Habitat

Nilgiri Langurs inhabit moist evergreen, semi-evergreen, and montane Shola forests of the Western Ghats at altitudes ranging from 300 m to over 2,000 m above sea level.

Montane Shola Forests

High-altitude stunted evergreen forest patches nestled in valleys between rolling grasslands.

Upper Rainforest Canopy

Tall mature canopy trees providing food, sleeping branches, and aerial travel paths.

Humid Riverine Belts

Streamside vegetation offering high leaf diversity and year-round moisture availability.

Distribution

Regional Occurrence

Endemic to the hill ranges of South India across three states:

  • Tamil Nadu: Nilgiri Hills, Anamalai Hills, Palani Hills, Meghamalai.
  • Kerala: Silent Valley, Wayanad, Periyar, Agasthyamalai, Ponmudi.
  • Karnataka: Brahmagiri Hills, Kodagu, Pushpagiri Wildlife Sanctuary.

Wild Population Estimate

~10,000 – 15,000

Estimated remaining wild population across protected reserves in the Southern Western Ghats.

Behaviour

Group Structure & Sociality

They form social troops of 8 to 12 individuals consisting of a dominant adult male, multiple adult females, and offspring. Solitary all-male bands also roam home ranges of 50 to 100 hectares.

Vocalizations & Territory

The male produces a resonant, low-pitched "whoop-whoop" call at dawn and dusk to signal troop presence and advertise territorial boundaries across steep forest valleys.

Diet

Strictly herbivorous with a strong preference for young leaves, flowers, seeds, and fruits of evergreen trees.

Conservation Status

Threats

Poaching for Native Medicine

Historically hunted for its pelt, flesh, and organs misbelieved in traditional systems to possess medicinal or aphrodisiac properties.

Plantation Encroachment

Conversion of primary rainforests into tea, coffee, cardamom, and eucalyptus monocultures fragments habitat.

Roadkills & Power Lines

Highways cutting through reserves lead to vehicle strikes when langurs descend to cross open road gaps.

Key Protected Areas

Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve

India's first biosphere reserve, encompassing Mudumalai, Bandipur, Silent Valley, and Nagarhole.

Periyar Tiger Reserve

Protected evergreen forests around Periyar Lake supporting large, stable langur troops.

Kalakad Mundanthurai (KMTR)

South Western Ghats stronghold with continuous rainforest canopy corridors.

Interesting Facts

01

Bacterial Fermentation: Their sacculated stomach works like a cow's rumen to break down leaf cellulose and detoxify plant compounds.

02

Loud Whoop Calls: A single territorial call by an alpha male can resound across 1–2 kilometres of mountain terrain.

03

Golden Infants: Newborn infants are born with reddish-brown coat color that gradually darkens into glossy black over several months.

04

Canopy Acrobatics: They can bound across 10-metre gaps between high canopy branches with exceptional agility.
