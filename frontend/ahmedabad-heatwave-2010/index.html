The 2010 Ahmedabad Heatwave

The event that gave South Asia its first Heat Action Plan

In May 2010, Ahmedabad recorded a peak temperature of 46.8°C and an estimated 1,300+ excess deaths in a single month. The scale of the loss exposed how unprepared Indian cities were for extreme heat — and directly led, three years later, to South Asia's first coordinated Heat Action Plan: an early-warning and response system that has since been credited with saving over a thousand lives every year.

📖 Event Context

Ahmedabad, a fast-growing city in western Gujarat, has always experienced hot, dry summers — but May 2010 was unusual even by local standards. A prolonged spell of extreme heat settled over the city, intensified by a slow-moving weather system, and pushed temperatures well past the seasonal norm for over a week. Hospitals reported being overwhelmed with heatstroke and dehydration cases, and reports from the period describe birds and bats dying and falling from trees in the heat — a striking sign of how severe conditions had become for the wider urban ecosystem, not just people.

🌡️ Temperature Conditions

Temperatures in Ahmedabad reached a documented high of 46.8°C during May 2010, with the most intense stretch of heat occurring between 19–25 May. A peer-reviewed mortality study found daily maximum temperature was strongly correlated with daily death counts across April (r = 0.69), May (r = 0.77), and June (r = 0.39) of that year — confirming a direct, measurable relationship between the heat and the rising death toll, not just a seasonal coincidence.

Measuring the Mortality Spike

During the most intense week (19–25 May 2010), researchers calculated mortality rate ratios of up to 2.12 compared with earlier reference periods — meaning the death rate was more than double what would normally be expected for that week.

🏙️ The Urban Heat Environment

Ahmedabad's rapid urbanisation compounded the natural heat load. Dense construction, dark asphalt roads, and a shrinking share of green and open space create a classic urban heat island effect, where city centres trap and re-radiate heat well after sunset. Public health researchers later pointed out a key monitoring gap: official temperature readings came from a single weather station near the airport, which does not capture the higher heat experienced in the dense city core — where surface temperatures on tarmac, where people actually walk and work, can run around 10°C hotter than the official reading. Low-income neighbourhoods and informal slum housing, often built with poorly insulating materials and minimal ventilation, bore a disproportionate share of the indoor heat exposure.

🏥 Public-Health Impacts

A peer-reviewed excess-mortality study compared all-cause deaths in May 2010 against matched reference periods from May 2009 and 2011. It found 4,462 all-cause deaths occurred in May 2010, an excess of roughly 1,344 deaths — a 43.1% increase over the expected baseline of 3,118 deaths. Other contemporary estimates cited in follow-up research and reporting place the toll at over 1,300 excess deaths, with some accounts of the single most intense week alone citing around 800 deaths. Vulnerable groups — the elderly, outdoor labourers, and slum-dwelling households without reliable cooling or shade — were disproportionately affected, as hospitals across the city struggled to keep pace with heatstroke and dehydration cases.

📋 Heat Action Plan Development

In direct response to the 2010 disaster, the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation (AMC) partnered with the Indian Institute of Public Health, Gandhinagar (IIPH-G), the Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC), the Public Health Foundation of India, and researchers from Emory University and the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai to build a formal response plan.

The resulting Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan (HAP) launched on 16 April 2013 — the first comprehensive, city-specific heat early-warning and preparedness plan in South Asia. It has since been regularly updated (through at least a sixth edition) and has served as a template that other Indian cities and states have adapted for their own heat plans.

Four Core Strategies

The HAP is built around: (1) building public awareness, (2) training medical professionals to recognise and treat heat illness, (3) an inter-agency early-warning and alert system, and (4) targeted efforts to reduce heat exposure — including nodal-officer coordination across municipal departments.

🚨 Early-Warning Systems

The plan's early-warning system runs on a colour-coded alert scale tied to forecast temperatures from the India Meteorological Department: orange alerts for daily highs above roughly 40°C ("very hot") and red alerts for highs above roughly 45°C ("extremely hot"). A designated nodal officer within the AMC issues these alerts using a 7-day probabilistic weather forecast, triggering the plan in advance of a dangerous heat spell rather than reacting after the fact. Warnings are pushed out through electronic LED display boards, SMS messages, local media, hoardings, and print advertisements, so the message reaches both officials and the general public with several days' notice.

🤝 Community Preparedness

  • Cool Roofs Initiative — grassroots group Mahila Housing Trust coats the roofs of low-income households with heat-reflective paint, lowering indoor temperatures by up to 5°C; over 100 government buildings have also been retrofitted this way.
  • Heat wards in hospitals — dedicated triage units for heatstroke, dehydration, and heat-related illness, staffed by trained medical personnel.
  • Outreach to vulnerable groups — targeted training and awareness programmes for slum communities, outdoor workers, and school children.
  • Public cooling measures — mist sprinklers at traffic signals, extended access to drinking water points, and buttermilk distribution during peak heat hours.
  • Heat insurance for women workers — a 2024 pilot by Mahila Housing Trust pays women a cash benefit when minimum temperatures cross a set trigger, encouraging them to skip outdoor work safely without losing income.

🎓 Lessons Learned

Independent evaluations found the Ahmedabad HAP prevented an estimated 1,190 deaths per year in its first five years, and roughly 2,380 deaths between 2014 and 2015 alone. Its success has been attributed to a simple, science-based, and partnership-driven design: it combines hard infrastructure (cool roofs), clear risk communication (colour-coded alerts), and cross-agency coordination, rather than relying on any single fix. The model's replicability has been its biggest legacy — heat early-warning systems and action plans, built on Ahmedabad's framework, have since scaled to roughly 30 cities across 11 Indian states, coordinated with the National Disaster Management Authority and the India Meteorological Department. A recurring critique from later research is that plans in other cities have inconsistently adopted proven elements like cool roofs and urban-heat-island analysis — underscoring that Ahmedabad's full model, not just the alert system, is what drives its results.
