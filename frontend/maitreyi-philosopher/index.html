Brihadaranyaka Upanishad · c. 700 BCE

मैत्रेयीMaitreyi

The philosopher who asked her husband the one question wealth cannot answer.

"What should I do with that by which I do not become immortal?" Maitreyi, to Yajnavalkya — Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.3
Who was she

A seer in her own right

Maitreyi was a philosopher of the later Vedic period, remembered as one of two wives of the sage Yajnavalkya. Tradition names her a brahmavadini — a woman versed in the Vedas and capable of expounding sacred knowledge in her own voice, not merely receiving it.

Later commentators also credit her with a handful of hymns within the Rig Veda, placing her among a small number of named women associated with the composition of Vedic verse. What has kept her name alive for nearly three thousand years, however, is not a hymn but a single conversation: the moment she turned down half a fortune to ask her husband a harder question.

Setting the scene

The world of the forest schools

Maitreyi's story unfolds in the closing centuries of the Vedic period, roughly the eighth century BCE, in the forest hermitages (ashramas) of northern India where teachers and their households lived, studied, and debated. This was a world organised around the household fire and the sacrifice, but also one where philosophical inquiry — into what is real, what survives death, and what a self actually is — was becoming its own discipline, separate from ritual.

Her husband Yajnavalkya appears throughout the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad as a formidable court philosopher at the assembly of King Janaka of Videha, known for defeating rival scholars in public debate. It is at the point where he decides to leave household life to become a wandering renunciant — dividing his property between his two wives, Maitreyi and Katyayani — that the dialogue with Maitreyi takes place.

The text names her home life plainly: two wives, a household, property to divide. It is against this very ordinary domestic backdrop that Maitreyi asks a question that is anything but ordinary.

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, 2.4 & 4.5

The dialogue with Yajnavalkya

Preserved in two closely related passages of the Upanishad, this exchange is staged as a husband's farewell to his household — and a wife's refusal to let the farewell stay small talk.

Yajnavalkya

"I am about to leave this household life. Let me settle what is due between you and Katyayani, before I go."

He proposes to divide his estate between his two wives as he prepares to renounce worldly life.

Maitreyi

"If this whole earth, full of wealth, were mine — would it make me immortal?"

She turns the conversation from property to the one thing property cannot buy.

Yajnavalkya

"No. Your life would be like the life of the wealthy. There is no hope of immortality through riches."

He answers her honestly, without softening it — comfort is not the same as immortality.

Maitreyi

"Then what use is it to me? Tell me instead, sir, of the only thing you know that leads to immortality."

She declines her share of the estate and asks to be taught instead.

Yajnavalkya

"You have always been dear to me — and dearer still now, for asking this. Sit, and I will explain. Reflect deeply on what I say."

Her question earns her a full philosophical teaching on the nature of the Self, love, and what it means to know something truly.

The central theme

Knowledge and immortality

The dialogue's force comes from a contrast it draws directly, in Maitreyi's own words: wealth secures comfort, but only knowledge of the Self is said to touch what is deathless.

What wealth offers

Comfort, not immortality

Yajnavalkya's answer is direct: owning "the whole earth full of wealth" would make Maitreyi's life resemble that of any rich person — secure, comfortable, and still bound by the same mortality as everyone else. Wealth changes circumstance; it does not change fate.

What knowledge offers

A different kind of security

Because wealth cannot answer her real question, Maitreyi asks to be taught instead. The Upanishad presents this as the more valuable inheritance — not property divided between two wives, but a teaching that, once understood, cannot be divided, spent, or lost.

सा होवाच मैत्रेयी, येनाहं नामृता स्यां किमहं तेन कुर्याम्? यदेव भगवान् वेद तदेव मे ब्रूहीति॥
sā hovāca maitreyī, yenāhaṃ nāmṛtā syāṃ kimahaṃ tena kuryām? yadeva bhagavān veda tadeva me brūhīti ||
Translation: Maitreyi said: "What should I do with that by which I do not become immortal? What you know, revered sir, that alone tell me." (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.4.3)
न वा अरे पत्युः कामाय पतिः प्रियो भवति, आत्मनस्तु कामाय पतिः प्रियो भवति...
na vā are patyuḥ kāmāya patiḥ priyo bhavati, ātmanas tu kāmāya patiḥ priyo bhavati...
Translation: "Not for the sake of the husband is the husband dear, but for the sake of the Self (Atman) is the husband dear... Not for the sake of all is all dear, but for the sake of the Self is all dear." (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.4.5)
The teaching itself

So what did Yajnavalkya teach her?

Yajnavalkya's teaching centres on Atman — usually translated as "Self," though not the everyday personality. Rather than a possession the self has, it is described as the innermost awareness underlying every experience: the "seer" that cannot itself be fully seen, because it is doing the seeing.

He tells Maitreyi that nothing in life is loved for its own sake — not a spouse, a child, or wealth — but because the Self is present within it. Turn attention toward that Self directly, the teaching goes, and one comes to understand everything else through it. This claim, and the closely related idea that Atman and the universal principle Brahman are ultimately one, became a founding reference point for the school of thought later known as Advaita ("non-dual") Vedanta.

The Threefold Method of Realisation (*Sādhana-Trayam*)

Yajnavalkya gives Maitreyi the classic Upanishadic formula for Self-knowledge: "आत्मा वा अरे द्रष्टव्यः श्रोतव्यो मन्तव्यो निदिध्यासितव्यः" (The Self should verily be realized — heard, reflected upon, and deeply meditated upon):

1. Śravaṇa

Hearing the Sacred Truth

Listening with undivided attention to the wisdom of the Upanishads from an illumined teacher, dispelling doubts regarding the nature of reality.

2. Manana

Logical Reflection

Rigorously reflecting upon the teachings through reason and dialectic inquiry, removing all conceptual contradictions in one's own understanding.

3. Nididhyāsana

Profound Meditation

Unbroken contemplative absorption on the non-dual Truth until conceptual knowledge transforms into direct, living experiential awareness.

Why it mattered later

Philosophical legacy

The Maitreyi–Yajnavalkya exchange did not stay confined to the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad. It was influential enough that the ninth-century philosopher Sureshvara, a direct disciple of the Advaita Vedanta founder Adi Shankara, wrote an extended verse commentary (vartika) on it — treating Maitreyi's question as a serious philosophical opening rather than a domestic aside.

Within the history of Indian philosophy, the passage is often read as an early, vivid statement of a recurring theme: that self-knowledge, not accumulation, is what philosophy is ultimately for. Maitreyi is remembered less as a passive listener than as the person whose question shapes the entire teaching that follows.

Textual evidence vs. later interpretation

What the text itself supports: The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (chapters 2.4 and 4.5) is the earliest and firmest source for Maitreyi — her question about wealth and immortality, her request to be taught, and Yajnavalkya's teaching on the Self are recorded there directly, in Sanskrit, and are the basis for every later account.

What comes from later tradition: Details such as her authorship of specific Rig Vedic hymns, biographical elaborations of her life before and after this dialogue, and her status as a philosophical exemplar for women come mainly from later commentaries, devotional literature, and modern retellings. These traditions are historically significant in their own right — they show how later generations read and honoured her — but they sit a layer removed from the Upanishadic text itself, and different sources do not always agree on the details.

Wider context

Women in ancient Indian thought

Maitreyi was not the only woman recorded taking part in Vedic-era philosophical life — and her memory has continued to shape how that history is told.

Gargi Vachaknavi

Another woman philosopher who appears in the very same Upanishad, Gargi famously challenges Yajnavalkya with a rapid sequence of questions at King Janaka's court — one of the few named instances of a woman debating openly in that setting.

The brahmavadini tradition

Texts from this period recognise a category of women — brahmavadinis — trained in Vedic learning and able to teach it. Maitreyi and Gargi are the two most frequently cited examples of this tradition in the Upanishads.

A lasting reference point

Maitreyi is regularly cited in discussions of women's education in ancient India, and is often treated as a symbol of intellectual seriousness rather than a marginal figure in someone else's story.

Her name today

Her memory continues into the present: Maitreyi College in New Delhi, a constituent college of the University of Delhi, is named in her honour.

Chronology

A historical timeline

Dates for this period are approximate and debated among scholars; they are given here as a general guide, not a precise record.

c. 800–700 BCE

Later Vedic period

The likely setting for Maitreyi's life: a world of forest hermitages, household ritual, and an emerging tradition of philosophical debate alongside the older sacrificial religion.

c. 700 BCE

Composition of the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad

One of the oldest and longest Upanishads takes shape, preserving the dialogue between Maitreyi and Yajnavalkya in chapter 2, section 4 — repeated with variation in chapter 4, section 5.

1st millennium BCE onward

Transmission within the Vedic canon

The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad is preserved and transmitted as part of the Shukla Yajurveda tradition, studied and memorised across generations of teachers and students.

c. 8th–9th century CE

Shankara's commentary (bhashya)

Adi Shankara, founder of Advaita Vedanta, writes an influential commentary on the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, shaping how the Maitreyi dialogue would be read for centuries afterward.

9th century CE

Sureshvara's vartika

Shankara's disciple Sureshvara composes a dedicated verse commentary on the Maitreyi–Yajnavalkya section, treating it as a distinct philosophical text worth study in its own right.

20th century CE

Maitreyi College founded

A women's college of the University of Delhi is named after her, carrying her association with learning and philosophical seriousness into modern Indian education.
