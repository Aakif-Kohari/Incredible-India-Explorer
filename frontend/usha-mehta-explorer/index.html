🇮🇳 Freedom Fighter & Underground Broadcaster

Usha Mehta Voice of the Secret Congress Radio & Heroine of the 1942 Movement

Lifespan: 25 March 1920 – 11 August 2000 • Birthplace: Saras village, Surat, Gujarat • Award: Padma Vibhushan (1998)

When the British arrested Mahatma Gandhi and top national leaders overnight on 9 August 1942, 22-year-old student Usha Mehta stepped forward to give the leaderless Quit India Movement its voice. From clandestine rooms across Bombay, she operated the secret Congress Radio on 42.34 metres — broadcasting uncensored truth to a defiant nation until her arrest.

Early Life & Principles

A Life Formed by Gandhian Defiance

Childhood in Saras & First Meeting with Gandhi

Usha Mehta was born on 25 March 1920 in the village of Saras, near Olpad in Surat district, Gujarat. Her father, Hariprasad Mehta, was a judge under British rule, which made her early childhood politically quiet. However, when Usha was just eight years old in 1928, she visited Mahatma Gandhi's ashram at Ahmedabad. The meeting left an indelible mark on her young mind, inspiring her to embrace Khadi and commit to national service.

Later that year, when her father retired and the family moved to Bombay, young Usha enthusiastically joined protest marches against the British-appointed Simon Commission. Participating in child brigades (*Vanar Sena*), she marched through the streets shouting slogans against colonial rule, undeterred by police warnings.

Education at Wilson College & Vow of Celibacy

Usha was an exceptionally bright student. She completed her schooling in Kheda and Bombay, and subsequently enrolled at the prestigious Wilson College, Bombay. She graduated in 1939 with a first-class degree in Philosophy, and began studying law at Government Law College.

Deeply inspired by Mahatma Gandhi's ideals of *satyagraha*, self-discipline, and simplicity, Usha took a vow of celibacy (*brahmacharya*) and dedicated herself to living in Khadi. When World War II broke out and the freedom struggle intensified, she put her law studies on hold to devote her whole self to the independence movement.

"We did not look upon Congress Radio as a political weapon alone; it was our sacred duty to carry the Voice of Freedom to every home when darkness was forced upon the land."
— Dr. Usha Mehta

42.34 Metres Wavelength

The Secret Congress Radio Operation (1942)

Conception & Core Underground Network

On 8 August 1942, Mahatma Gandhi gave the historic call to "Do or Die" at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay. Before dawn on 9 August, the British colonial authorities launched Operation Zero Hour, arresting Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel, and virtually the entire Congress Working Committee. A complete press censorship was imposed across India.

Refusing to let the movement be silenced, 22-year-old Usha Mehta joined hands with associates Babubhai Thakkar, Vithalbhai Jhaveri, Chandrakant Jhaveri, and socialist leader Dr. Ram Manohar Lohia. With technical assistance from Nariman A. Printer and transmitter equipment quietly supplied by Nanka Motwani (owner of Chicago Radio), they assembled a 10-watt shortwave radio transmitter.

Evading Mobile Detector Vans Across Bombay

On 27 August 1942, the secret station broadcast for the very first time with the legendary announcement:
"This is the Congress Radio calling on 42.34 metres from somewhere in India."

To prevent the British Special Branch and mobile direction-finding detection vans from pinpointing their signal, the team constantly moved the heavy transmitter equipment between clandestine locations across Bombay:

  • Sea-View Building (Chowpatty)
  • Bombay Mutual Building (Fort)
  • Laxmi Building (Lamington Road)
  • Parekh Wadi (Girgaon Back Road)

Raid & Resistance

The Raid at Parekh Wadi & Solitary Confinement

The Raid on 12 November 1942

For nearly three months (88 days), Congress Radio outsmarted British intelligence agencies. However, technical adviser Nariman Printer was eventually apprehended by police and coerced into revealing details. On the night of 12 November 1942, British police officers led by Inspector Kokje broke down the door of Room 106 at Parekh Wadi, Girgaon Back Road.

Even as the police battered the door, Usha Mehta calmly continued the broadcast, playing the recording of *Vande Mataram* until the very last note before being placed under arrest along with Chandrakant Jhaveri.

Solitary Confinement & Trial at Yerwada Jail

Following her arrest, Usha was held in solitary confinement for six months at the CID headquarters in Bombay. Senior police officers subjected her to grueling interrogations day and night, offering lucrative rewards, government scholarships abroad, and full immunity if she disclosed the names of her underground colleagues (such as Dr. Ram Manohar Lohia and Achyut Patwardhan). Usha maintained complete silence.

During her trial before the Special Judge, Usha refused to answer questions that would compromise the movement. In April 1943, she was sentenced to 4 years' rigorous imprisonment and transferred to **Yerwada Central Jail** in Pune. She served her sentence bravely until her release in April 1946.

Academic Life & Honors

Post-Independence Career & National Honors

Academic Excellence & University of Mumbai

Following her release in 1946 and India's independence in 1947, Usha Mehta resumed her education. She earned her Ph.D. in Political Philosophy from the University of Bombay with a pioneering dissertation on *The Political and Social Thought of Mahatma Gandhi*.

She joined the University of Mumbai as a faculty member, eventually rising to become Professor and Head of the Department of Civics and Politics. She trained generations of political scientists, authored numerous scholarly books, and served as President of the Gandhi Smarak Nidhi.

Padma Vibhushan & National Remembrance

In 1998, on the occasion of the 50th anniversary of Indian Independence, the Government of India conferred upon Dr. Usha Mehta the **Padma Vibhushan**, India's second-highest civilian honor.

Usha Mehta passed away on 11 August 2000 at the age of 80. Her legacy lives on as a shining beacon of courage, integrity, and press freedom in India's struggle for self-determination.
