The Light of Punjab

Who Was Guru Nanak?

📜

Historical Evidence

What scholars can confirm

Guru Nanak Dev Ji (1469–1539 CE) is the founder of Sikhism and the first of the ten Sikh Gurus. His life and teachings are preserved primarily in the Guru Granth Sahib (974 hymns composed by him), the Janamsakhis (hagiographic accounts), and the writings of early Sikh historians like Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana. Most scholars place his active period between c. 1499–1539 CE, during which he undertook four major journeys known as Udasis.

  • Born at Talwandi (now Nankana Sahib, Pakistan)
  • 1469–1539 CE
  • 974 hymns in Guru Granth Sahib
  • Four major Udasis covering vast distances
🙏

Traditional Accounts

Stories passed through devotion

The Janamsakhis — traditional hagiographic accounts composed centuries after Guru Nanak's lifetime — narrate miraculous events: a meteor shower at his birth, his refusal to eat until given Hindu and Muslim food simultaneously, and his three-day disappearance into the Kali Bein river after which he emerged with the proclamation "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim". These narratives, while cherished by devotees, are considered later traditional accounts rather than verified history.

  • Birthplace: Talwandi / Nankana Sahib
  • Raised by father Mehta Kalu Chand and mother Mata Tripta
  • Childhood teacher: Pandit Hardial
  • Married to Mata Sulakhani, father of sons Sri Chand and Lakhmi Chand
🚶‍♂️

What Are the Udasis?

The word "Udasi" comes from the Sanskrit udāsīna (उदासीन), meaning "detached" or "indifferent to worldly affairs." Guru Nanak's Udasis were extensive spiritual journeys undertaken between c. 1499 and 1521 CE, covering an estimated 28,000+ kilometres across South Asia, Central Asia, and possibly the Middle East. He travelled on foot, accompanied by his lifelong companion Bhai Mardana (a Muslim musician), visiting Hindu temples, Muslim mosques, Buddhist monasteries, and Jain centres — engaging with people of every faith, caste, and social standing.

Distinguishing History from Tradition

The exact routes, dates, and specific encounters of the Udasis vary significantly across different Janamsakhi traditions (Puratan, Bala, Mani Singh, and Mehrban versions). Modern scholarship considers the Puratan Janamsakhi (the oldest extant version) as more reliable, though even it was composed over a century after Guru Nanak's lifetime. The Guru Granth Sahib provides the most authoritative source for his teachings, while the specific details of his travels remain matters of faith and tradition.

Sacred Travels

The Four Major Udasis

Guru Nanak's journeys took him in four cardinal directions from Punjab — each Udasi carrying a distinct spiritual purpose and covering vast territories.

East
🌅

First Udasi — Eastward

c. 1499–1502 CE

Guru Nanak journeyed east through Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Bengal, and Assam, visiting the holy cities of Haridwar, Varanasi, Gaya, and Dhaka. At Haridwar, he challenged priests who claimed water offerings could reach ancestors in heaven. In Bengal, he encountered Buddhist monks and discussed the nature of the divine. He reached the far-eastern frontier of Assam, engaging with tribal communities.

The Puratan Janamsakhi records his debate at Haridwar where he cast water eastward, declaring "I water my fields in Punjab — will they grow here?"
South
🏛️

Second Udasi — Southward

c. 1502–1506 CE

Travelling south through Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu, Guru Nanak visited the great temples of Puri, Rameswaram, and Madurai. He met Jain monks in Shravanabelagola, Hindu pandits in Madurai, and Sufi saints along the Konkan coast. In Ceylon (Sri Lanka), he reportedly climbed Adam's Peak (Sri Pada), a sacred site for multiple faiths.

In Madurai, tradition records Guru Nanak's debate with Shaivite priests about the nature of Shiva and the formless divine.
North
🏔️

Third Udasi — Northward

c. 1506–1514 CE

The longest and most arduous journey took Guru Nanak through Kashmir, Ladakh, Tibet, and Central Asia. He traversed mountain passes, visited Buddhist monasteries, and engaged with Tibetan lamas. Some Janamsakhis record his journey reaching as far as Mecca and Medina in Arabia, where he is said to have debated with Muslim clerics about the nature of prayer and the direction of worship.

The Mecca narrative — where Guru Nanak allegedly fell asleep with his feet towards the Kaaba — is one of the most celebrated stories in Sikh tradition.
West
🏺

Fourth Udasi — Westward

c. 1514–1521 CE

Guru Nanak's final major journey took him through Sindh, Balochistan, and possibly into Persia (Iran) and Afghanistan. He visited the Sufi shrines of Multan, engaged with Muslim scholars in Baghdad, and traversed the trade routes connecting India to the Islamic world. This Udasi cemented his reputation as a teacher whose message transcended all geographical and religious boundaries.

Some traditions record Guru Nanak visiting the shrine of Hazrat Baal in Baghdad, where his footprint is still preserved.

Eternal Wisdom

Core Teachings & Principles

During his Udasis, Guru Nanak shared revolutionary teachings that challenged the religious orthodoxy of his time and laid the foundation for Sikhism.

🙏

Ik Onkar (One God)

The fundamental declaration of Sikh faith — there is one Supreme Reality that pervades all creation. This formless, timeless, self-existent Being (Ik Onkar) is accessible to all through meditation, not through priests or rituals.

Core Doctrine
⚖️

Equality of All

Guru Nanak rejected the caste system, untouchability, and gender inequality. He declared "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" — insisting that before God, all humans are equal regardless of birth, creed, or social status.

Social Revolution
💫

Kirat Karo, Naam Japo, Wand Chhako

The three pillars of Sikh life: honest work (Kirat Karo), remembrance of God (Naam Japo), and sharing with others (Wand Chhako). A balanced life of work, worship, and charity.

Way of Life
🏛️

Rejection of Ritualism

Guru Nanak condemned empty pilgrimages, idol worship, fasting as mere show, and priestly hypocrisy. He declared the human body itself the true temple and meditation the true pilgrimage.

Spiritual Critique
🌍

Universal Brotherhood

His Udasis were a living demonstration of interfaith dialogue — visiting temples, mosques, monasteries, and shrines of every tradition, engaging with respect and sharing a message of shared divinity in all beings.

Unity of Faiths
🎵

The Power of Kirtan

Guru Nanak elevated devotional singing (Kirtan) as the highest form of worship. Accompanied by Bhai Mardana on the rebab, his hymns transformed the spiritual atmosphere of every place he visited.

Sacred Dialogues

Famous Encounters & Episodes

During his travels, Guru Nanak engaged in profound dialogues with religious leaders, rulers, and common people — each encounter carrying a timeless lesson.

💧

The Haridwar Water Debate

At Haridwar, priests scooped water towards the sun to offer it to deceased ancestors. Guru Nanak splashed water towards his fields in Punjab. When challenged, he declared: "If your water reaches heaven, surely my water will reach my crops!" — exposing the futility of mechanical ritual.

First Udasi — East
Kaaba

The Mecca Episode

Arriving at Mecca exhausted, Guru Nanak lay down to sleep with his feet pointing towards the Kaaba. The Qazis moved his feet towards the qibla — but the Kaaba miraculously moved with them. Guru Nanak awoke, declaring: "The whole earth is God's house — which way shall I turn?"

Third Udasi — North
🦁

Encounter with Emperor Babur

When Babur's Mughal army invaded Punjab in 1520, Guru Nanak was imprisoned along with other captives. His hymns during imprisonment — the Babur Vani — lamented the suffering caused by tyranny and affirmed that God's justice ultimately prevails over earthly power.

Historical
🐍

The Serpent at Kurkshetra

At Kurkshetra, Guru Nanak noticed a Brahmin performing rites for his dead father, dropping balls of rice wrapped in cloth. When the rice balls rolled towards Guru Nanak, he declared this a sign that the father's soul was not at peace — demonstrating the futility of empty ritual without true devotion.

In Sindh, Guru Nanak met a merchant who had gained wealth through dishonest means but sought spiritual merit. Guru Nanak's verse, the "Gajni", challenged him to earn wealth through honest labour and share it generously — establishing the principle of Kirat Karo (honest work).

Fourth Udasi — West
🌻

The Story of Dharmdas

In Bengal, Guru Nanak met a devout Brahmin named Dharmdas who served food to all travellers. Guru Nanak blessed him, saying his devotion through service was more pleasing to God than any amount of ritual worship — establishing Seva (selfless service) as a core Sikh value.

Wider Perspective

Historical & Cultural Context

⚔️

Medieval India — A Divided World

Guru Nanak lived during a period of profound religious and political upheaval in India. The Delhi Sultanate had recently given way to the Mughal Empire under Babur (1526). Hindu-Muslim tensions, caste oppression, and religious ritualism created deep social divisions. Guru Nanak's message of universal love and equality was a direct response to this fractured society.

🕌

The Bhakti & Sufi Traditions

Guru Nanak's Udasis coincided with the flourishing of the Bhakti movement in Hinduism and the Sufi tradition in Islam. Saints like Kabir, Ravidas, and Farid were contemporaries. Guru Nanak engaged with both traditions, drawing on their emphasis on personal devotion while pushing beyond their sectarian boundaries.

🗺️

The Geography of the Udasis

The Udasis covered an extraordinary range of territories: from the Himalayan peaks of Ladakh to the tropical coasts of Kerala, from the deserts of Sindh to the forests of Bengal, and possibly as far as Mecca and Baghdad. This vast geographical reach made Guru Nanak's message truly pan-Asian in scope.

📚

Sources & Scholarship

Key scholarly works on Guru Nanak's Udasis include Pashaura Singh's "The Guru Granth Sahib: Canon, Meaning and Authority," O.P. Bharadwaj's studies on the Janamsakhis, and H.R. Gupta's "History of the Sikhs." The Puratan Janamsakhi remains the primary traditional source, while archaeological and historical evidence continues to illuminate the context of his travels.
