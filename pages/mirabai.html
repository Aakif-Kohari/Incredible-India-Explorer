The Princess Who Became a Saint

Who Was Mirabai?

📜

Historical Evidence

What scholars can confirm

Mirabai (c. 1498–1546 CE) is one of the most celebrated poet-saints of the Bhakti movement. She was born into the Rajput clan of Chauhans in the Kingdom of Merta (present-day Rajasthan). Her compositions — over 1,300 bhajans attributed to her — are preserved in manuscripts across Rajasthan and Gujarat. While the exact details of her life are debated among historians, her poetry and devotional legacy are universally acknowledged.

  • Born in Merta, Kingdom of Marwar (Rajasthan)
  • c. 1498–1546 CE (approximate)
  • 1,300+ bhajans attributed to her
  • Chauhan Rajput clan of Chomu
🙏

Traditional Accounts

Stories passed through devotion

The hagiographic tradition — particularly the Meera Bai Padavali and various Rajasthani folk narratives — tells of a princess who, from childhood, was obsessed with a Krishna idol (murti) gifted by a wandering sage. Refusing to worship anyone but Krishna, she defied her royal in-laws, survived poisoning attempts, walked through fire, and ultimately vanished into the sanctum of a temple in Dwarka. These narratives, while cherished by devotees, are considered later traditional accounts.

  • Childhood devotion to Krishna idol
  • Married to Prince Bhojraj of Mewar
  • Disciple of saint Raidas (Ravidas)
  • Final disappearance at Dwarka temple
🦚

Why Is She Called "The Poet of Devotion"?

Mirabai composed her bhajans in Rajasthani, Braj Bhasha, and Hindi — languages accessible to common people, not the Sanskrit of Brahmin scholars. Her poetry expressed a raw, personal, and often scandalously intimate relationship with Krishna. She addressed God as her husband, lover, and companion — roles that were reserved for mortal men in 16th-century Rajput society. This "mad devotion" (prem bhakti) made her both a beloved figure and a source of controversy among orthodox religious authorities.

Distinguishing History from Tradition

The most widely known narrative of Mirabai's life comes from later literary and folk traditions, particularly the Meera Kavya (composed c. 17th century) and the Rajasthani Padavali collections. Modern scholars like Linda Hess and Rosalind Lefeber have noted that while her devotion to Krishna and her status as a poet-saint are well-established, many specific biographical details (the poisoning, the fire ordeal, the disappearance) are hagiographic embellishments rather than verified history.

Chronicle of a Saint

The Life of Mirabai

From childhood visions to royal defiance to final absorption in the divine — Mirabai's life was a living poem.

👶

Childhood in Merta

c. 1498–1510 CE

Born in the kingdom of Merta, Mirabai was a Chauhan Rajput princess. According to tradition, her father gifted her a Krishna idol as a child, and she became inseparable from it. She refused to worship any other deity and preferred the company of wandering saints (sadhus) over royal courtiers. Her mother, who initially supported her devotion, died when Mirabai was young.

Some traditions say she heard Krishna's flute in the wind from the age of five — a call that would define her entire life.
💍

Marriage to Prince Bhojraj

c. 1510 CE

Mirabai was married to Kunwar Bhojraj, the prince of Mewar (Udaipur), in a political alliance between the kingdoms of Merta and Mewar. She became a princess in one of Rajputana's most prestigious courts. However, her devotion to Krishna and her refusal to perform traditional royal duties — including worshipping her husband as a deity — created immediate tension with the Mewar royal family.

In Rajput tradition, the wife was expected to treat her husband as God. Mirabai's devotion to Krishna alone was considered scandalous.
🔥

Defiance & Persecution

c. 1510–1530 CE

After Bhojraj's death, Mirabai refused to perform sati (self-immolation) and instead devoted herself entirely to Krishna. She sang bhajans publicly, wore simple clothes, and visited temples — behaviour considered disgraceful for a Rajput widow. According to tradition, her in-laws tried to kill her by sending her a poisoned cup, which she drank after offering it to Krishna, remaining unharmed.

The poisoning narrative symbolises the idea that true devotion renders one immune to the malice of the world.
🚶‍♀️

Wandering & Pilgrimage

c. 1530–1540 CE

Mirabai left the palace and wandered across Rajasthan, Gujarat, and eventually to Dwarka — the legendary kingdom of Krishna on the western coast. She lived among saints, performed kirtan (devotional singing), and composed hundreds of bhajans. Her wanderings took her through Vrindavan, Mathura, and Pushkar — the holiest sites of Krishna worship.

Her disciples included both upper-caste Brahmins and low-caste devotees — a radical challenge to the caste system.
🕉️

Absorption at Dwarka

c. 1546 CE

The most famous end to Mirabai's story: she entered the temple of Dwarkadheesh (Krishna) at Dwarka, and when she did not emerge, her followers broke down the door — only to find the idol of Krishna standing in her place, and Mirabai nowhere to be seen. This divine absorption (sankeerthan) became the ultimate symbol of her complete union with Krishna.

This narrative parallels similar stories of saints merging with their beloved deity — a recurring motif in Bhakti literature.
📖

Literary Legacy

c. 1546 CE onwards

Mirabai's bhajans were collected and preserved by her followers, particularly the Rajasthani Padavali tradition. Over 1,300 compositions are attributed to her, though scholars debate the authenticity of many. Her most famous bhajan, "Payo Ji Maine Ram Ratan Dhan Payo", is sung across India. Her poetry blends personal ecstasy with philosophical depth, making her one of India's most beloved poet-saints.

Her compositions appear in the Guru Granth Sahib (1 verse), the Bijak, and numerous Rajasthani manuscripts.

Divine Love

Mirabai's Krishna Devotion

Mirabai's relationship with Krishna was not mere worship — it was a consuming, revolutionary love that dissolved all social boundaries.

💙

Sakhi Bhava — The Friend Relationship

Mirabai addressed Krishna as her intimate friend and companion. Unlike the formal worship of temples, she spoke to Krishna as one speaks to a beloved — with familiarity, playfulness, and raw honesty. This "sakhi bhava" (friendship-devotion) made her bhajans deeply personal and relatable.

Core Practice
🔥

Mad Devotion — The Pyaasa Meta

Mira called herself "Pyaasa" — the thirsty one, burning with longing for Krishna. Her poetry is filled with images of fire, thirst, and the pain of separation (viraha). This intense, almost obsessive devotion scandalised Rajput society but inspired millions of later devotees.

Emotional Core
⚖️

Social Rebellion Through Love

By declaring Krishna her true husband, Mirabai rejected the patriarchal structures of Rajput society. She refused to perform sati, challenged Brahmin priests, and insisted that her relationship with God superseded all worldly obligations. Her devotion was simultaneously spiritual and radical.

Revolution
🎵

The Power of Bhajan

Mirabai elevated devotional singing (bhajan) as the highest form of worship. She sang publicly in streets and temples — an activity considered inappropriate for Rajput women. Her bhajans became her identity, her weapon, and her prayer all at once.

Devotional Practice
🌊

The Viraha Tradition

Many of Mirabai's most powerful poems express viraha — the agony of separation from the beloved. Borrowed from the Sanskrit literary tradition, she transformed it from romantic poetry into a vehicle for spiritual longing. The pain of absence became the fuel for devotion.

Poetic Tradition
🏛️

The Nirguna–Saguna Bridge

While Mirabai worshipped the saguna (personal, embodied) form of Krishna, her poetry often transcends form to touch the nirguna (formless) divine. This bridging of devotional approaches made her accessible to both Krishna worshippers and followers of formless devotion.

Enduring Influence

Cultural Legacy & Modern Resonance

🎭

Performing Arts

Mirabai's bhajans are central to Rajasthani folk music, Hindustani classical, and Bollywood. The iconic film Mirabai (1945) and the ballet Mirabai by Leela Samson brought her story to global audiences. Her compositions are performed at every major Indian classical music festival.

🎨

Visual Art & Miniatures

The Mewar school of miniature painting produced dozens of works depicting Mirabai — singing before Krishna's idol, walking with saints, defying her in-laws. These paintings, now in museums worldwide, created the iconic visual image of Mira as the white-clad devotee with her ektara (one-stringed instrument).

📚

Literary Influence

Mirabai influenced poets from Tulsidas to Kabir to modern writers like Sarojini Naidu and A.K. Ramanujan. The scholar Linda Hess translated her works into English, bringing her to academic and literary audiences globally. Her poetry appears in the Guru Granth Sahib and the Bijak.

🌍

Global Recognition

Mirabai has been celebrated by Western scholars, musicians, and feminists. The Mirabai Project and various international music collaborations have performed her bhajans across the globe. She is studied in women's studies, religious studies, and South Asian literature courses worldwide.

🏛️

Pilgrimage Sites

Key sites associated with Mirabai — the Mirabai Temple at Chittorgarh, the Dwarka temple, and Vrindavan — are major pilgrimage destinations. The Mirabai Memorial in Chittorgarh attracts thousands of devotees annually.

👩

Feminist Icon

Mirabai is celebrated as one of India's earliest feminist voices. Her refusal to submit to patriarchal norms — choosing divine love over royal duty, public singing over silent obedience — has made her a symbol of women's spiritual independence and resistance across centuries.

Wider Perspective

Historical & Cultural Context

⚔️

Medieval Rajputana

Mirabai lived during a turbulent period in Rajputana. The Mughal Empire under Akbar was expanding into Rajasthan, and Rajput kingdoms were caught between loyalty to Hindu traditions and accommodation with Mughal power. Her husband Bhojraj fought against the Mughals. In this context of political upheaval, Mirabai's spiritual revolution was equally destabilising to social order.

🕌

The Bhakti Movement

Mirabai was a product and pioneer of the Bhakti movement that swept across India between the 14th and 17th centuries. She was a contemporary of Kabir, Ravidas, Tulsidas, and Surdas. Her unique contribution was bringing Bhakti into the heart of Rajput royalty — proving that devotion could flourish even within the rigid structures of feudal power.

🎵

The Bhajan Tradition

Mirabai's bhajans belong to the broader tradition of devotional song-poems that emerged across India. The Rajasthani Padavali tradition — a literary form combining verse with musical notation — preserved her works through oral and manuscript transmission. Her compositions blend Rajasthani folk, Braj Bhasha, and Hindi linguistic traditions.

📚

Sources & Scholarship

Key scholarly works include Linda Hess's "The Bijak of Kabir" (which discusses Mirabai's relationship to Kabir), Rosalind Lefeber's "Songs of the Saints from the Adi Granth," and Vijay Pincha's studies on the Meera Padavali manuscripts. The Padmini Rajappa edition of Mirabai's collected works remains the standard scholarly reference.
